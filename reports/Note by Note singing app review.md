# Note by Note hears well, judges poorly

Note by Note is a genuinely good foundation: its pitch detector reads clean voice-like tones to within about a cent, it moves every lesson into your own key and octave, and its default "hear it, then sing it back" format is the same echo task researchers use to retrain adults who sing out of tune, all free, private and account-free in a market where comparable structured apps are paid subscriptions. The weak part is the judge, not the ear. The scoring counts the natural gaps between notes as misses, calls wide vibrato "wobble", gives crisp staccato 22% while the same exercise sung legato gets 92%, blames wrong notes on "singing sharp", and a labelling bug shows the wrong do-re-mi syllables in every lesson that changes key; those numbers flow straight into "Copy results for Claude", so the AI coach inherits the errors. Research on how skills are learned points to a second gap: the app only ever measures you with live feedback on, and although singing studies disagree on whether that feedback helps or hinders later, none supports reading high scores with the line on as proof you can sing without it. The app also has almost no vocal-health guardrails: nothing says stop if it hurts, the warm-up sirens reach both ends of your range within the first minute, and the streak counts a silent 0% run as a day of practice. The recommended order starts with a 10-minute test on your own iPhone, then small fixes to labels, scoring and safety wording, then medium builds (note-level scoring, "without the line" checks, a progress history, hard-spot practice), then large ones (an adaptive path, technique lessons that need new measurement, a sturdier detector). No phone app can judge tone, breath or strain, so a few lessons with a human teacher remain the best complement, with Claude most useful as a practice planner fed richer and more honest data.

*How to read this report.* It draws on a code audit ([code audit](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/app_audit_code.md)), a hands-on walkthrough with synthetic voices and 124 screenshots ([live audit](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/app_audit_live.md)), and eight research reviews covering technique, pitch science, practice design, vocal health, ear training, range and repertoire, competing apps and pitch-detection technology. Most published studies were read through their abstracts because publisher sites blocked full texts, and **no study has tested a phone singing app with a delayed "can you still do it tomorrow" check**, so several recommendations are well-reasoned extrapolations rather than proven recipes. Each recommendation carries an evidence-strength label for that reason. "Cents" measure pitch distance: 100 cents is one half step, the distance between neighbouring piano keys.

## The foundation already matches what research says beginners need

The single most useful research finding for an adult beginner is that the problem is rarely the ear. In lab testing **about 85–90% of occasional singers sing in tune**, yet about 60% of more than 1,000 university students believed they could not imitate a melody; the 10–15% who are measurably inaccurate can hear pitch differences normally and instead struggle to translate a heard pitch into the right vocal movement ([Pfordresher & Brown 2007](http://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf)). True tone deafness (congenital amusia) affects roughly **1.5%** of people ([Pfordresher & Demorest 2021](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343166548_The_Prevalence_and_Correlates_of_Accurate_Singing)), and about 17% of Western adults wrongly call themselves tone deaf ([Sloboda, Wise & Peretz 2005](https://nyaspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1196/annals.1360.018)). Singing in tune is a trainable ear-to-voice coordination skill, and an app built around hearing a note and copying it is aimed at exactly the right target.

Note by Note's default format is the right one. With headphones off (the default), each exercise plays the pattern, gives one tick, and then you sing in silence ([code audit §1.2](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/app_audit_code.md)). That is the standard research paradigm for building the ear-to-voice link, and it matters that singing *with* the guide is not the default: poor-pitch singers "appeared to be hindered rather than helped by singing with correct accompaniment" ([Pfordresher & Brown 2007](http://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf)). The closest published evidence to this app is encouraging: adults with poor pitch who trained with visual pitch feedback in an iPad app improved significantly while audio-only and control groups did not, with the gain concentrated on **4-note melodies** rather than single notes ([Berglin, Pfordresher & Demorest 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/03057356211026730)), close to the short patterns in the app's Steps lesson and echo game. Wilson's group reported beginners benefiting from a rich scrolling contour display like the app's pitch lane, but also that display complexity adds mental load, and accounts of the study (known only from summaries) conflict, so an uncluttered lane is the safer design ([Wilson et al. 2008](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/251823345_Learning_to_sing_in_tune_Does_real-time_visual_feedback_help)).

The engineering underneath is sound. In the code audit's simulation the detector was **within 1 cent from 65 Hz to 1,245 Hz** on synthetic voice-like tones ([code audit §3](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/app_audit_code.md)); in the live run, noise alone never produced a false pitch and a lower-voice run with notes around 140–220 Hz tracked fine ([live audit §2](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/app_audit_live.md)). Published tests of a variant of the same algorithm (YIN) on studio recordings of singing report fine-pitch errors of **8–15 cents** ([Babacan et al. 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.12609)), comfortably inside any sensible "in tune" band, so the detector is not what holds the app back. Every lesson and song is transposed into your measured range and the guide plays in your own octave; octave slips are forgiven with an explanatory message, which fits both choir practice (men naturally answer a woman's note an octave lower ([Rowbury](https://blog.chrisrowbury.com/2016/05/how-male-singers-can-successfully-pitch.html))) and the finding that people match targets that resemble their own voice best ([Hutchins & Peretz 2012](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51603187_A_Frog_in_Your_Throat_or_in_Your_Ear_Searching_for_the_Causes_of_Poor_Singing)). The range test asks for a *comfortable* low and high note ("Low, but not growly", "Not a squeak"), which is safer than consumer tests that chase extremes and matches voice-science guidance to start low and never push ([VoiceScience](https://www.voicescience.org/articles/how-to-test-your-vocal-range/)).

The content choices are also defensible. The early sequence (match, hold, steps, five-note scale, full scale, leaps, arpeggios, minor, echo game) moves from single notes to patterns sensibly. The daily warm-up of sirens, five-note scale, arpeggio and long notes mirrors the exercises singers most commonly report using, though at about three minutes it is shorter than the 5–10 minutes those singers prefer ([Gish et al. 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21439780/)); in one study of voice students 5 or 10 minutes eased singing more than none or 15, other work suggests a few minutes suffice, and a choir study found the most in-tune singing after a combined physical and vocal warm-up ([duration study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32891479/); [U Miami](https://news.med.miami.edu/miller-school-researchers-study-ideal-vocal-warm-up-time-for-singers/); [choral warm-ups](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28483445/)). Its opening sirens on "ng" or a lip trill are semi-occluded exercises (partly closed mouth: humming, lip trills, straws), which head Ingo Titze's list of warm-ups and have a lab-tested rationale for making the voice easier to start and sustain ([Titze, "Five Best Warm-Ups"](https://vocology.utah.edu/_resources/documents/titzes_top_five_vocal_warm_ups.pdf); [J Voice 2025 straw study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12353171/)). The line-by-line song mode with syllables printed on the note bars works well in practice ([live audit §3.13](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/app_audit_live.md)). Commercially, structured singing apps are mostly paid subscriptions of roughly US$55–120 a year, and the free options lack a curriculum ([American Songwriter](https://americansongwriter.com/best-singing-apps/); [VocalRefresh, vendor](https://vocalrefresh.com/alternatives/simply-sing)); some new "AI coach" apps work by uploading your recordings ([Bloom Vocal, vendor](https://www.bloomvocal.site/en/blog/singing-coach-ai-review-2026)). A free, range-fitted, on-device course with a "Copy results for Claude" coaching hook is a genuine niche.

## Scoring currently punishes healthy singing and misreads mistakes

The app scores every video frame (about 60 per second) against a tight band, and that design produces confident, wrong verdicts. **Vibrato** is the clearest case. Natural singing vibrato typically swings about 50–120 cents at 4.5–6.5 times a second ([VoiceScience, vibrato](https://www.voicescience.org/lexicon/vibrato/)), and listeners hear a vibrato note at roughly its centre ([J Acoust Soc Jpn 1983](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ast1980/4/2/4_2_73/_pdf)). The default "in tune" band is only ±30 cents, so a perfectly centred ±50-cent vibrato scored **72%, "35% held steady", with the tip "Your long notes wobbled. Breathe low into your belly"** ([screenshot 22](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/screenshots/22-results-hold-vibrato-50c.png)). The same singer on Match a note was told to "aim to land each note sooner" although every entry was on time. Worse, the range test demands that 85% of frames sit within half a semitone, so a ±60-cent vibrato **never completes the very first screen**: the hold bar peaked at 38% and sat there for 25 seconds with no explanation ([screenshot](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/screenshots/08c-range-test-vib60-stuck.png)).

The headline percentage mostly measures **how much of each bar you filled with sound**, not how well you tuned. After a 0.2-second grace period every silent frame inside a note counts as a miss, so breaths, consonants and natural releases between short notes cost accurate singers **6–25 points**. Waltzing Matilda sung with all **91 of 91 notes landed and a 6-cent average miss scored 77%**, with the tip to "aim to land each note sooner" even though every entry was within about a tenth of a second ([live audit §2](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/app_audit_live.md)). The Short and sharp lesson inverts its own instruction: crisp 100 ms staccato notes scored **22%, 0 of 15 landed, "The mic lost you… sing a little louder"** ([live audit §3.11](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/app_audit_live.md)), a more realistic 160 ms staccato landed all 15 notes with a 0-cent miss yet scored **61%** ([screenshot 29f](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/screenshots/29f-results-staccato.png)), and singing each note long and joined up scored **92% "Spot on"** ([29g](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/screenshots/29g-results-staccato-sung-legato.png)). Two identical synthetic runs of the Five-note scale scored 87% "Spot on" and 85% "Nicely done": scores vary by a couple of points from run to run, and because the verdict uses the unrounded score, a displayed 85% can still read "Nicely done" ([live audit §3.14](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/app_audit_live.md); `app.js` `resultsSheet`).

The tips misdiagnose because the "lean" and "average miss" numbers average every frame, wrong notes included. A run of wrong notes produced **"On average you sat about 61 cents sharp. Relax your jaw"** when the singer was simply singing different notes ([screenshot 19](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/screenshots/19-results-copy-fallback-textarea.png)). Research on how experts actually judge untrained singers points to a better model: across **166 untrained singers rated by 18 experts, interval errors, contour errors and key drift together explained 81% of the judges' scores** ([Larrouy-Maestri et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23280380/)). People hear notes and the steps between them, not frame averages. Research also separates *accuracy* (your average distance from the target, which the app reports) from *precision* (how consistent you are from attempt to attempt, which it does not), and imprecision is the more widespread problem ([Pfordresher et al. 2010](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article/128/4/2182/683506/Imprecise-singing-is-widespread)). The app's "held steady" figure measures wobble within one note, not consistency across notes.

Two outright bugs compound this. First, **in every exercise that climbs through several keys, the note labels, scale-degree colours and do-re-mi syllables stay stuck in the first key**, because the pitch lane is drawn from the first key only (`modelFor` in `app.js` reads `step.tonic`, which `lessons.js` sets to the first rung of the key ladder). In key 2 of the Five-note scale, do-re-mi mode shows **"fa, fi, le" while you are singing mi, fa, sol** ([screenshot 35](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/screenshots/35-lesson-five-solfa-key2-wrong-syllables.png)). This affects seven lessons (Steps, Five-note, Full scale, Leaps, Arpeggios, Minor, Short and sharp) plus the warm-up's scale and arpeggio, and it actively teaches a solfa learner the wrong syllables. Second, the microphone's noise gate slowly learns any steady sound as "background", so in simulation a quiet held note (below roughly 60% on the in-app level bar) was **dropped after 1.3–3.3 seconds at 60 Hz and after 0.6–1.7 seconds on a 120 Hz iPhone screen**; the note then scores zero and the tip wrongly says "sing louder" ([code audit §7.1](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/app_audit_code.md)). The code comment claims the opposite, and the real-world impact needs an iPhone test, but the logic flaw is certain and it hits exactly the Hold, Longer holds, Sirens and warm-up long notes.

This matters beyond fairness. In reviews of competing apps, unreliable pitch and range detection is the complaint that most erodes trust ([Top Consumer Reviews](https://www.topconsumerreviews.com/best-singing-lessons/reviews/yousician.php); [JustUseApp reviews of Simply Sing](https://justuseapp.com/en/app/1592900217/simply-sing-by-joytunes/reviews)). Note by Note's detector is trustworthy; its verdicts currently are not, and the misdiagnosed lean, the wrong key spellings and a mislabelled voice type all travel into the text you paste into Claude.

## High scores with the pitch line on do not prove you can sing without it

The most important research insight for the app's design is the difference between *performance* and *learning*. Motor-learning research calls it the guidance hypothesis: feedback given during every attempt improves performance while it is on, but learners can come to lean on it instead of building their own internal sense, so performance drops when it is removed ([Maas et al. 2008](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1058-0360(2008/025))). The singing evidence is mixed rather than a clean replication. In a **10-week randomised study of adult non-music students, both groups improved but the group with constant visual pitch feedback was not significantly better at post-test**; the claim that its advantage during training vanished once feedback was withdrawn comes from one secondary summary ([Paney & Tharp 2021](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334113826_The_effect_of_concurrent_visual_feedback_on_adult_singing_accuracy)). Other studies point the other way: in Berglin's study only the visual-feedback group improved, and Wilson's group found accuracy dipped *while* singers watched the display and rose above baseline afterwards ([Wilson et al. 2008](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/251823345_Learning_to_sing_in_tune_Does_real-time_visual_feedback_help)). A 2026 systematic review of 36 studies proposes that live feedback boosts in-session performance but risks "guidance dependency", while feedback after the attempt retains better; 29 of those studies never tested that pathway, so this is a theoretical synthesis rather than a pooled result ([Zhang 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1920074/full)). Note by Note gives live feedback on every attempt (the trace, the dot's colour, bars that fill as you hit them, and an "In tune" or "N¢ flat" readout), never tests you without it, and records only your best score with it on; the replay on the results sheet is its one piece of after-the-attempt feedback. Its progress badges therefore measure the easier thing. No study has tested the exact remedy (hide the line on some attempts, fade it as you improve) for singing, so the case for it is a hedge against a plausible risk, borrowed from speech and general motor learning, and a direct measure of the real goal.

The app also has **no memory of how you are doing**. Every lesson is open from day one, "Next" follows a fixed order whatever you scored, tempos and tolerances never change, the warm-up is identical every day, and history is limited to best score, last score and run count ([code audit §6](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/app_audit_code.md)). Each of those runs against the evidence, though most of it comes from classrooms and instrumental music. Mastery learning (advance once a criterion is met, with extra help otherwise) raised course-exam scores by about half a standard deviation, a sizeable gain, across **108 controlled evaluations** in classroom academic courses, with the biggest gains for weaker learners; the effect on standardised tests was only about a tenth of that size, and self-paced versions gained less ([Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns 1990](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543060002265)). Sleep matters: in one music study only the group whose practice sessions were **24 hours apart** improved significantly ([Simmons 2012](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ951332)). And *how* you practise beats how much: among 17 advanced pianists, practice time did not predict next-day retention, but the best performers fixed errors immediately, pinpointed their source and systematically slowed down problem spots ([Duke, Simmons & Cash 2009](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429408328851)). The app cannot do any of that: results never point you to your worst note or offer a slow drill of it, and each song runs start to finish as one scored block, with **no way to loop a hard line, slow the tempo or learn the tune before the words** ([code audit §1.5](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/app_audit_code.md)). Two findings support those song tools directly: most occasional singers sang **as accurately as professionals when the tempo was slowed** ([Dalla Bella, Giguère & Peretz 2007](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/6457528_Singing_proficiency_in_the_general_population)), and 39 occasional singers made fewer interval and contour errors singing familiar tunes on "la" than with the lyrics ([Berkowska & Dalla Bella 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19673763/)), though in young children text helped ([Educ Sci 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/educsci15080984)).

The range and key logic is too literal. The app captures **one self-chosen "comfortable" note at each end, from a cold voice**, treats that pair as the whole working band, and never prompts a re-test ([code audit §2](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/app_audit_code.md)). A timid beginner will give a narrow range, and poor-pitch singers restrict their matching range for reasons that are not physical; in one training study only the group practising across a **full octave, rather than a fifth, improved significantly**, and only on single-note matching, not four-note melodies ([Pfordresher & Greenspon 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10298649241289542), result known only from search summaries). Measured top and bottom notes are reliable on retest ([dual-microphone VRP study](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2017_JSLHR-S-16-0460)) and trained singers have wider measured ranges than untrained ones (a cross-sectional comparison: [Sulter, Schutte & Miller 1995](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199705801985)), so a gentle slide to find the extremes, plus periodic re-tests, would anchor everything more firmly. The "Closest voice type" label is computed from the midpoint of the two notes, so **a man singing C3 to C5 is labelled "Alto"**; the classical pedagogues Richard Miller and James McKinney both warn that range is the least reliable basis for classifying beginners and that early labels create false expectations ([summary of Miller and McKinney](https://www.researchcatalogue.net/view/3711502/3711501)), and at least one user of a competing app disputes such a label ([JustUseApp](https://justuseapp.com/en/app/1592900217/simply-sing-by-joytunes/reviews)). Songs are centred by their lowest-to-highest span, whereas teachers and church music directors judge a key by **where the melody spends its time** and keep the top safely inside the comfortable zone ([Reformed Worship](https://reformedworship.org/resource/highs-and-lows-singing)); Waltzing Matilda's 17-semitone span exactly fills a 17-semitone range, and on a narrow range such as G3–D4, Match, Full scale, Arpeggios, the Echo game and seven of eight songs silently run outside it.

The sound around the exercises is thin. Match a note, Hold it steady, Sirens and Short and sharp play **no key-setting chord at all**, and where chords are played they sit an octave below the tonic: **roughly 55–125 Hz for the Lower-voice preset, lower still for basses**, far below what a phone speaker reproduces well ([code audit §5](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/app_audit_code.md); `lessons.js` `triad`). A sense of key helps people perceive and remember intervals ([Graves & Oxenham 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01753/full)), and among 198 high-school sight-singers the strong ones established the key before singing far more often, though that is a correlation ([Killian & Henry 2005](https://doi.org/10.1177/002242940505300105)). The research does not support a continuous drone: players' measured tuning did not improve with one, even though most believed it did ([Springer et al. 2021](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1311395)). A short key-setting cue before singing is the better-supported option, but no study has tested whether it improves sung accuracy in adult beginners. The guide itself is a synthesised buzzy tone; 19 women matched a female voice more accurately than instrument timbres ([Watts & Hall 2008](https://doi.org/10.1080/14015430802028434)), though no comparable study of men was found and evidence on human vocal models for adults is inconsistent ([J Voice](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199706001603)).

Technique teaching beyond pitch is almost absent. Classical and contemporary traditions agree a beginner needs a balanced onset (neither breathy nor hard), easy breath coordination, vowel and resonance work and a smooth passage between chest and head voice ([Miller, *The Structure of Singing*](https://archive.org/details/structureofsingi0000mill); [LoVetri, Somatic Voicework](https://somaticvoicework.com/strategies/)). Note by Note offers sirens, holds and a staccato "ha" drill (Short and sharp), but no balanced-onset exercise and no onset scoring, no messa di voce (swelling and fading on one note, a core control exercise in Titze's list), and almost every pattern climbs from the bottom, whereas teachers commonly use **light descending "oo" and "ee" patterns** to stop beginners dragging their chest voice upwards (tradition rather than trial evidence). The wording of the tips also leans on body instructions ("Breathe low into your belly", "Let your belly kick each one out"). Breathing studies find large differences between good singers and no support for one prescribed abdominal pattern ([Salomoni et al. 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0155084)), while untrained singers produced better tone when told to aim the sound at a point on the far wall than when told to feel their throat ([Atkins & Duke 2013](https://acda-publications.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/IJRCS/volumefour/ijrcs4_2_atkinsduke.pdf)).

Finally, motivation rests on a consecutive-day streak that counts any finished run, including a silent 0% one. Intact streaks do motivate, but a broken streak demotivates, especially when people blame themselves, and that harm shrinks when a streak can be repaired ([Silverman & Barasch 2023](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/49/6/1095/6623414)); missing a single day does not derail habit formation, which took a median of 66 days in one study of 96 volunteers ([Lally et al. 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.674)). A small team that built a "Duolingo for singing" found users saying "I keep my streak going but I don't think I'm actually getting better" ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/max_polaczuk/we-tried-to-build-duolingo-for-singing-with-a-tiny-team-heres-what-actually-worked-4k61), anecdote). The app sensibly has no leaderboards; a 16-week classroom study linked a course with leaderboards *and* badges to lower intrinsic motivation and exam scores ([Hanus & Fox 2015](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/265644737_Assessing_the_effects_of_gamification_in_the_classroom_A_longitudinal_study_on_intrinsic_motivation_social_comparison_satisfaction_effort_and_academic_performance)), which also argues for keeping the app's best-score badges as information rather than rewards. What it lacks is visible evidence that you are improving week to week.

## Vocal-health guardrails and a dozen bugs need attention first

**Nothing in the app tells you to stop if singing hurts.** The safety-adjacent wording is limited to "Low, but not growly", "Not a squeak", "Nudge the edges if they felt like a stretch", "Keep the top note light" and "Redo the range test if the notes felt uncomfortable" ([code audit §1.4, §2](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/app_audit_code.md)), whereas voice-science guidance for range testing says plainly: "If you feel any pain, strain, or discomfort during testing, stop immediately and rest your voice" ([VoiceScience](https://www.voicescience.org/articles/how-to-test-your-vocal-range/)). Teachers stress that an app "can't feel the discomfort you feel", so repeating exercises while straining does harm ([Kunstplaza](https://www.kunstplaza.de/en/music/learn-to-sing-app-singing-teachers-comparison/), teacher opinion). The sirens do widen gradually, through 55%, 78% and 100% of the space around the middle of your range, but because they are the warm-up's first step, their third round reaches **within about one semitone of both ends of your range in the first minute** ([code audit §1.3](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/app_audit_code.md)). In a study of 10 sopranos, a short warm-up actually *raised* the pressure needed to start the voice at high pitches ([Motel et al. 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12825648/)); it did not compare glide orders, but it is a reason for caution near the top, and reaching the edges last is teaching tradition rather than a tested rule ([vocal-health notes §4](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/vocal_health_and_warmups.md)). Because the stored comfortable range is also the working band, there is no safety margin under the top note, and narrow ranges overflow without warning.

The app also has no idea how much you have sung. There is **no established safe daily dose** of singing ([NCVS](https://ncvs.org/strategies-for-safety-thresholds-of-phonation-for-performers-via-dosimetry/)), but after a two-hour reading-aloud task voices recovered about 90% within 4–6 hours and fully within 12–18 hours ([Hunter & Titze 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3311979/)), and spikes in vocal load were followed by worse self-ratings for **24–72 hours** in classical singers ([Carroll et al. 2006](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0194599806027835)). A daily streak with no rest or "unwell" days nudges you to sing while hoarse, and singing too soon after laryngitis can prolong it ([patient.info](https://patient.info/ears-nose-throat-mouth/laryngitis-leaflet)). The clinical rule worth putting in the app: **hoarseness that has not improved within 4 weeks should be examined, or sooner if a serious cause is suspected** (the 2018 US otolaryngology guideline, which replaced an older three-month allowance) ([AAFP summary](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2018/1115/p606.html)). The guideline's serious-cause red flags reportedly include a smoking history, a neck lump, recent surgery or intubation and noisy breathing, and sudden voice loss after loud singing is a standard laryngology warning sign, but none of these could be checked against the guideline text or another source here, so the list must be verified before the app quotes it ([vocal-health notes §3](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/vocal_health_and_warmups.md)). In Australia the route is a GP, who can refer to an ENT, or a speech pathologist directly ([healthdirect](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/amp/article/speech-pathology)). Any hygiene tips should stay evidence-true: sip water, dry air makes singing feel harder, and do not tell people to avoid coffee, since a systematic review found little evidence caffeine harms the voice ([J Voice 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0892199721000849)). Warm-ups should be described as making singing feel easier, and possibly steadier, not as preventing injury or fixing pitch: no study has measured injury, and the pitch evidence conflicts ([choral warm-ups](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28483445/); [J Voice 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199723000747)).

## A three-tier plan: fix the judge, fade the crutches, then adapt

The ordering below puts trust and safety first, then the changes with the best evidence per unit of effort, then larger builds. Several things should deliberately stay as they are, because the research backs them: hear-then-sing as the default, "comfortable" wording in the range test, octave forgiveness, semi-occluded sirens at the start of the warm-up, short daily sessions, no leaderboards, and on-device privacy. Evidence strength is labelled **Strong** (replicated peer-reviewed findings), **Moderate** (one or two good studies or strong theory), **Limited** (small, indirect or borrowed from other fields), **Tradition** (teaching consensus without trials) or **Bug fix** (needs no research). Sizes are rough and assume you build with Claude: **small** is about one working session, **medium** a few sessions plus checks on your iPhone, **large** many sessions. Until the fixes land, you can work around the worst problems: set Settings → How strict to **Relaxed** and treat the percentage as rough; use letter names rather than do-re-mi in lessons that change key; hold long notes at a solid medium volume with the phone close; ignore the "wobbled" and "land each note sooner" tips; and if the top of the warm-up feels like a stretch, retest your range and nudge the high note down a semitone or two.

### What no app can do, and where a teacher or Claude fits

A phone microphone is reliable for pitch and everything derived from it: accuracy, consistency, drift on long notes, scooping into notes, vibrato speed and width, breaks in a slide, and timing. Loudness is usable only in relative terms. Voice-quality measures degrade badly with device and room differences; studies disagree on everything except pitch, and one standard quality measure fell significantly in ordinary exam-room noise ([JSLHR 2024](https://doi.org/10.1044/2024_jslhr-23-00759); [JSLHR 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33186510/)). Even lab software misjudges hard vocal onsets by **up to 71 ms** on clean recordings ([VOAT 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/2306-5354/13/8/950)). Breathiness versus pressed tone, jaw and tongue tension, posture, resonance, chest-versus-head quality and early strain are therefore out of reach, and apps that claim to detect "vocal tension" offer no evidence ([PitchFlow listing](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.pitchflow.vocal)). Note by Note should say plainly that it measures pitch and timing only; that honesty is itself a differentiator.

This is where a human teacher earns their place. McKinney's classic diagnostic method relies on "discriminatory hearing assisted by acute observation" ([vocalpedagogy.com](https://vocalpedagogy.com/vocal-pedagogue/james-mckinney/)): an ear and an eye on your body. Teachers commonly recommend apps for daily warm-ups, pitch matching and consistency, and a teacher for technique ([Preply](https://preply.com/en/blog/best-singing-learning-apps/)). For an adult beginner that suggests a handful of lessons early on to check for strain and set up easy onsets and breathing, then occasional check-ins, with the app carrying the daily pitch work. Persistent hoarseness belongs with a GP or speech pathologist, not a teacher or an app.

Claude fits best as a practice planner and explainer: it can read your numbers, spot patterns across runs, suggest what to practise next and explain concepts. It cannot hear you, knows only what the export contains, and there is no peer-reviewed evaluation of chatbot singing coaches. Today's export feeds it a flat/sharp "lean" that can be distorted by wrong notes, a possibly mislabelled voice type and wrong key spellings, and it leaves out headphone mode, key, tempo, vowel and any history ([code audit §4](../research_notes/Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review/app_audit_code.md)). A better export (N10) would open with a scope line ("This app measures pitch and timing only; please don't comment on tone, breath or tension, and don't diagnose; if I mention pain or hoarseness lasting weeks, suggest a GP or speech pathologist"), then give the note-level results from N1: consistency, wrong notes listed as wrong notes, tendencies by scale degree and by low, middle and high part of the range, whether vibrato was detected, with-line versus without-line scores, the audio route, key, tempo and vowel for each step, the last several runs of the same lesson, minutes sung today and the voice-feel answer. With that, Claude can reason the way a teacher does from symptom to likely cause, for example "your misses cluster at the top of your range" rather than "you are an alto who sings sharp".

## Conclusion

The central finding is that Note by Note is at risk of optimising for the wrong number rather than lacking features. Its percentage currently rewards sustained sound and performance with the pitch line on, so a trained singer's vibrato, a crisp staccato and a natural breath all look like failure, while a fast-improving beginner gets no proof that the skill is sticking. Redefining success as "notes centred in tune, consistently, without the line, on a later day" would realign almost everything else: what the Next button suggests, what the streak celebrates, what the home screen shows as progress and what Claude is told. Most of the evidence-backed upgrades change how existing exercises are scored, sequenced and displayed, or add lessons the existing builders can already generate, which makes them unusually cheap for a small free app.

For you as the singer, the practical implication is to run the 10-minute iPhone test first, because it decides which of the scoring fixes matter for your voice, and to stop whenever anything feels strained. Once the scoring is trustworthy, the app is well placed to do something competitors rarely do: test its own teaching ideas, such as a silent "hear it in your head" beat or a faded guide, against its own no-line, next-day data, and report honestly what worked.
