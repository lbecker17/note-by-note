# Keep the kids' singing on the phone

All four ideas are worth building inside the existing iPhone web app, at little or no cost. One line separates safe from risky: what stays on the child's phone is cheap, private and legally low-risk, while anything that leaves it (a copyrighted song on the public website, a child's voice on a company's server) brings licence fees, age limits and privacy law. **Voice support: yes.** Play short pre-made spoken prompts through the app's own sound engine, behind a per-phone "Read to me" switch and a replay button, and keep them off the pitch line. Skip voice commands, which do not work in Home Screen web apps. **More songs: yes, but not by licensing pop songs.** APRA AMCOS's cheapest online licence (**A$275 a year**) may not fit an app where children pick songs, and changed lyrics or new arrangements need each publisher's permission. Grow the shared library with songs out of copyright in both Australia and the US, family-written songs and suitable Creative Commons songs. Add a private "Family songs" editor whose copyrighted favourites never leave the phone. **Free sing: yes** to recording, playback, showing the tune as notes and a "sing it again" game. Give no percentage grade to children too young to hold a key (under about **5 or 6**). **A real song in their own voice: yes, on the phone.** First the child's tune plays back with chords and a beat; later their real recording goes over that backing. AI music services bar under-13s (Suno, Stability) or under-18s (ElevenLabs, Google, Kits.ai). Suno says it may treat a model of a user's singing voice as biometric data and keeps it for up to three years. **Build next:** a ten-minute phone check, self-hosted fonts and sound cues. Then build the note-finder and "play my tune as a song". Recording can start alongside once a small test page passes on the children's phones.

*How to read this report.* It draws on five research notes compiled on 1 October 2026 and builds on the earlier [Note by Note review](Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review.md). Many official pages (APRA AMCOS, Suno, WebKit, the OAIC) could only be read through search summaries. Confirm exact figures and terms on the linked page before relying on them. This matters most for AI-service terms, which changed several times in the last three months and are dated here. This is not legal advice. APRA AMCOS (02 9935 7900, licensing@apra.com.au, or medialicensing@apra.com.au for online services) and the Arts Law Centre of Australia can settle the grey areas, but the recommendations here stand whatever they say. Almost everything recommended is free and needs no server. Optional extras (Polly voice clips, MuseScore Pro, a custom domain) are priced where they appear. Effort uses the earlier review's scale and assumes you build with Claude. **Small** is about one working session, **medium** is a few sessions plus checks on the children's phones, and **large** is many sessions. These sizes are judgements, not prototypes. Three terms recur. The "Home Screen app" is the icon your children tap. The "public repo" is the app's code on GitHub, which anyone in the world can browse and download. The "share sheet" is the iPhone's Share menu (AirDrop, Messages, Save to Files).

## A few seconds of spoken prompt helps pre-readers; voice commands cannot work

**Recommendation: yes.** Add short spoken prompts for children who can't yet read well. Record them once as audio files and play them through the same sound engine as the app's piano. Put them behind a "Read to me" switch on each child's phone, with a speaker button that repeats the prompt. Skip voice commands entirely. This is among the cheapest high-value changes in this report. The app already shows the right words on screen ("Listen", "Your turn: sing on 'ah'") and already separates listening from singing (`js/app.js`). Narration only has to say those words at the right moments.

Design research on young children agrees on what good audio looks like. The Nielsen Norman Group's guidelines split children into pre-readers (3–5), beginning readers (6–8) and moderately skilled readers (9–12). For non-readers they recommend **a few seconds of clearly recorded audio**, after finding that children grew irritated when long spoken directions slowed them down ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/reports/children-on-the-web/)). Sesame Workshop's 2012 touchscreen guidance, drawn from more than 50 studies, says a friendly narrator should greet the child and state the goal, and that **every sound in the interface should have a specific meaning** ([Sesame Workshop](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/SesameWorkshop-2012.pdf)). Children do not pay reliable attention to audio alone, so a spoken prompt needs a visual cue alongside it. The key action word belongs at the end of the sentence ([Soni et al. 2019](https://init.cise.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/378/2019/04/TIDRC-Framework-soni-et-al-IDC19-final.pdf)): "When the line turns blue… sing!" works better than "Sing when the line turns blue". Leading apps follow this pattern. Khan Academy Kids lets children tap any text to hear it, and it marks right and wrong answers with a gentle "ding" and "bong" ([edu.com](https://www.edu.com/blog/understanding-khan-academy-kids-a-complete-guide-for-k-2-teachers-and-parents)). Sago Mini goes further: many of its apps play out with no words or narration at all ([Sago Mini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sago_Mini)). Two caveats apply. These findings come from summaries, because the reports themselves could not be opened. And no guideline written specifically for music apps for pre-readers turned up.

In a singing app the singing itself should be taught by sound, which Note by Note already does. Music teachers of young children "talk less, teach more". They sing a phrase for the child to echo rather than explaining it ([Frau Musik](https://fraumusik.com/talk-less-teach-more-music/); [MusicplayOnline](https://blog.musicplay.ca/helping-children-develop-the-singing-voice)). So narration should stick to navigation and framing: "Tap the big button", "Listen…", "Your turn!", "Great singing!". A short **sung two-note "your turn" motif** can replace the words altogether, with no reading, no translation and no licence needed. Whose voice to use depends on whether it speaks or sings. For spoken instructions, adult studies found that a modern computer voice produced as much learning as a human voice ([Craig & Schroeder 2017](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1016/j.compedu.2017.07.003)), though no matching study of 4–7-year-olds turned up. For sung examples, 282 children in grades 1–6 matched **a child's voice best, then an adult woman's, then an adult man's** ([Green 1990](https://doi.org/10.2307/3345186)). That matters only if the app ever swaps its synthesized guide note for recorded singing. It is also a reason not to have a parent record sung examples, since an off-pitch model would teach the wrong note. A parent recording spoken prompts carries no such risk.

On an iPhone, the obvious shortcut (the browser's built-in read-aloud voice) is the fragile option. It speaks only when started directly from a tap, it can freeze if asked to say an empty phrase, and the voice cannot be chosen reliably ([easy-speech issue](https://github.com/leaonline/easy-speech/issues/366); [Multi-Voice-Timer fix, September 2026](https://github.com/Outtech105k/Multi-Voice-Timer/pull/8); [talkrapp](https://talkrapp.com/speechSynthesis.html)). There is a worse problem for this app. Once the microphone and the app's sound engine are both running, iOS treats built-in speech as "system speech", which can come out quietly or from the earpiece, while sound played through the app's own engine stays on the loudspeaker ([Sam Eddy](https://samueleddy.com/writing/ios-safari-audio-sessions/)). Pre-made clips played the same way as the piano avoid all of these problems, play at the same volume as the notes and work offline. There are three ways to make them, each done once on a computer.

- **Free:** an open Australian-accented voice trained on public-domain (CC0) LibriVox narrators and fine-tuned from the "Jenny (Dioco)" Piper voice. Pre-made clips need no credit, though one is welcome. The project is tiny and nobody has reviewed its quality ([piper-en_AU](https://github.com/DataCraftsmanAustralia/piper-en_AU); [its attribution file](https://github.com/DataCraftsmanAustralia/piper-en_AU/blob/main/ATTRIBUTION.md)).
- **A few cents, plus an AWS account:** Amazon Polly, whose terms allow storing and redistributing the audio it generates ([Amazon Polly FAQ](https://aws.amazon.com/polly/faqs/)). Microsoft's and Google's voices are fine on paid plans, but whether their free tiers allow this use is disputed ([Microsoft Q&A](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/answers/questions/5805156/please-clarify-the-conflicting-information-regardi)). ElevenLabs' free plan forbids commercial use and requires a visible credit ([terms.law](https://terms.law/ai-output-rights/elevenlabs/)), and OpenAI requires telling listeners the voice is AI-generated ([OpenAI](https://platform.openai.com/docs/guides/text-to-speech)). One line in About ("Voice: computer-generated") covers any disclosure duty cheaply.
- **A parent's voice:** a parent records each prompt on each child's phone, which gives the youngest a familiar voice. Those recordings must stay on the phone and never go into the public repo, where anyone could download a family member's voice.

Start with the free voice. Have Claude generate three sample prompts, let the children listen, and switch to Polly only if they dislike it.

Keeping narration out of the singing needs one fix first. The app listens with echo cancellation switched off (`js/audio.js`). Anything coming out of the speaker while the mic is listening can therefore be drawn on the pitch line as if the child sang it, and a speaking voice has a clear pitch. Lessons already handle this for guide notes. Notes the child sings along to only sound when Headphones is on. Pitch readings during a "listen" phase, plus 0.3 seconds afterwards, are hidden (`js/lessons.js`, `js/app.js`). But that hiding lives only inside a lesson's timeline. Once the mic starts, nothing in `js/audio.js` ever stops it. Free sing draws every reading it gets, and it starts drawing the moment it opens if the mic was used earlier (`js/app.js`). The earlier review already lists the always-on mic as a bug ([earlier review, Q10](Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review.md)). Before narration ships, do one of two things. Either turn the mic off when a lesson or Free sing closes, as that review recommends. Or add one app-wide "narrating" flag that throws away pitch readings while any clip plays and for 0.3 seconds afterwards, on every screen including Free sing. Then three rules keep narration clean:

1. Speak only while the mic is off, during a lesson's "listen" phase, or under the narrating flag.
2. Cut the clip off the instant the child taps Start.
3. In Free sing, say one line before singing starts ("Tap the microphone, then sing anything you like!") and nothing while it is on.

No measurement of how much speaker sound leaks into an iPhone mic with echo cancellation off turned up. The phone check in the last section settles it in a few minutes.

Voice commands ("start", "again", "next") fail on two separate counts.

- **They don't run where the kids use the app.** The iPhone's web speech recognition works in Safari tabs but **is not available in web apps added to the Home Screen** ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/748048); [WebKit bug 225298](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=225298)), and that is how every child uses the app.
- **They can send speech away.** Safari's permission prompt says speech data is sent to Apple. Sources partly conflict on whether some recognition now runs on the phone ([Apple: Siri, Dictation & Privacy](https://www.apple.com/legal/privacy/data/en/ask-siri-dictation/); [addpipe](https://blog.addpipe.com/a-deep-dive-into-the-web-speech-api/)). Either way, it would break the app's on-screen promise that "Nothing is recorded or sent anywhere" (`js/app.js`).

Speech recognition is also still poor at understanding young children. A 2025 paper reports that voice-assistant technology "continues to underperform on children's speech" ([JASA Express Letters](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jel/article/5/3/035201/3338215/Voice-assistant-technology-continues-to)), and summaries of the research put error rates for younger children above 40% ([The Learning Agency](https://the-learning-agency.com/guides-resources/closing-the-child-speech-recognition-gap-evidence-limitations-and-paths-forward/)). A big "Again" button does the same job without any of these costs.

Because each child has their own iPhone, "Read to me" can be a simple per-phone setting. Turn it on for the 4–7-year-olds and leave it off by default for teens. Let any child who struggles with reading turn it on whatever their age. When it is on, each screen's prompt plays once when the child taps into that screen; the tap meets iOS's rule that sound must follow a touch. A speaker icon in the same corner of every screen replays the prompt, and the matching words on screen light up while they are spoken, so early readers link sound and text. These defaults are design judgement. No research on narration being on or off by default in mixed-age apps turned up, and it is worth watching whether a 4-year-old actually finds and uses the replay button.

| Option | Value for pre-readers | Effort | Cost | Privacy and legal risk | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sounds with a fixed meaning: a sung "your turn" motif, plus distinct sounds for listen, well done and try again | Medium–high; works without words, and teens won't mind | Small | A$0 | None | Build first |
| 20–40 pre-made spoken clips, a "Read to me" switch and a replay button, plus the mic fix above | High; the core fix | Medium | A$0 (open Australian voice) or cents plus an AWS account (Polly) | Low; add a "computer voice" note | Build second |
| Picture-first menus with fewer choices per screen | Medium; complements narration | Small–medium | A$0 | None | Alongside |
| Parent-recorded prompts, stored on each phone | High for the youngest | Medium–large (a recording screen, repeated on each phone) | A$0 | Low if never uploaded or put in the repo | Optional, later |
| Built-in read-aloud for text that changes, such as song names | Low–medium | Small but fragile | A$0 | None | Only while the mic is off |
| Voice commands | Low | Medium, and unavailable in Home Screen apps | A$0 | Medium: children's speech can go to Apple | Do not build |

The build order follows the table. Start with the sound cues: they need no recording or wording decisions, and they make "your turn" audible straight away. Next, write the prompt list (a few words each, matching the screen text, with the action word last), generate the clips once, and add the switch and replay button. Tidy the menus towards pictures at the same time. Leave parent-recorded prompts until the children have used the computer voice for a few weeks and you can see whether a familiar voice would help.

## Copyright risk lives on the public website, not on the family phone

**Recommendation: don't try to license popular songs.** Grow the shared library with songs that pass a two-country public-domain test, songs the family writes, and suitably licensed Creative Commons songs. Add a "Family songs" editor that lets a parent put a child's copyrighted favourites on that child's phone only. Never put a copyrighted melody or lyric into `songs.js`, the public repo or the website, "just for the kids" or otherwise.

A song normally carries two copyrights, one in the melody and one in the lyrics. When Note by Note includes a song, it copies both into its code, adds its own piano accompaniment, shows the words in time with the music, and makes all of it available through a public website and a public repo. Under Australian law, making material available online is "communicating" it to the public, which is one of the copyright owner's exclusive rights ([Victoria University](https://libraryguides.vu.edu.au/VUCopyright/definitions)). Australia has **no general fair-use exception** ([Copyright Agency](https://www.copyright.com.au/about-copyright/exceptions/)). Its private-copying exceptions only let people who own legitimate copies of particular formats, such as books or sound recordings, shift them into another format for home use ([ALRC](https://www.alrc.gov.au/publication/copyright-and-the-digital-economy-dp-79/9-private-and-domestic-use/current-law-2/)). None covers typing out lyrics or transcribing a melody, and none covers putting anything online. It makes no difference that only the family uses the app, because anyone can download a public repo. GitHub is a US service, so the realistic consequence is a US copyright takedown notice. GitHub contacts the repo owner, allows about **one business day** to remove the material, and publishes the notice in a public archive. A notice claiming that the whole repo infringes skips that window and disables the repo at once. GitHub can also close the accounts of repeat infringers, which would take the app's hosting with them ([GitHub DMCA policy](https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/content-removal-policies/dmca-takedown-policy)). Owners of Australian children's songs do go looking for unlicensed uses. Larrikin Music, which owns "Kookaburra Sits in the Old Gum Tree", won its case over "Down Under", and the Full Federal Court upheld the result in 2011 ([WIPO Lex](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/judgments/details/692)).

Licensing looks cheaper than it is. APRA AMCOS's **Online Mini licence** is for services earning under A$25,000 a year per activity. It costs **A$275, A$550 or A$1,100 a year for each category of use**, including GST, and has karaoke and backing-track categories. But it excludes interactive streaming where users choose songs, which is how Note by Note's song list works ([APRA AMCOS](https://www.apraamcos.com.au/music-licences/select-a-licence/online-mini-licence); [rate sheet](https://assets.apraamcos.com.au/images/PDFs/Music-Licences/PEG_online-mini.pdf)). Whether any of its karaoke categories covers on-screen lyrics could not be confirmed. What is clear is that changed lyrics and new arrangements, such as the app's own piano accompaniment, need permission from each song's publisher, and APRA AMCOS will supply the contact details ([APRA AMCOS FAQs](https://www.apraamcos.com.au/about/help/faqs)). Its guide for churches goes further: no AMCOS licence lets churches reproduce lyrics ([APRA AMCOS church guide](https://www.bbcatholic.org.au/ArticleDocuments/1566/Music-Copyright-Guide-for-Churches%20-%20APRA-AMCOS.pdf.aspx)). The lyrics industry also sells timed, karaoke-style lyrics as a separate product from static lyrics ([ProgrammableWeb on LyricFind](https://www.programmableweb.com/api/lyricfind)). Lyric services don't fill the gap. Musixmatch's free plan returns only **30% of each song's lyrics**, is non-commercial and requires running its tracking script, and commercial prices are available only on request ([FreeAPIHub](https://freeapihub.com/apis/musixmatch)). LyricFind licenses large platforms such as Amazon and YouTube ([LyricFind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LyricFind)) and publishes no price for small developers. Commercial apps work the same way. Yousician's catalogue depends on publisher agreements, and songs disappear when a licence expires ([Yousician](https://support.yousician.com/hc/en-us/articles/360000758937-Why-can-t-I-find-a-popular-song-in-Yousician)). Simply Sing's licensed songs sit behind a subscription of about **US$89.99 a year** ([Vocalify](https://vocalify.me/guides/simply-sing-alternatives.html)). For one much-loved song, writing to its publisher is the only legitimate way into the shared library, and the likely answer is a fee or no reply. A call to APRA AMCOS would settle whether a free family app fits any category, but nothing here depends on the answer.

There is plenty of safe ground. A song is safe for the public site when both its words and its music are out of copyright in **both Australia and the US**.

- **Australia:** two conditions must both hold. Every lyricist, composer and translator, and the arranger or editor of the version used, **died before 1 January 1955**. And the work was **published or made public before 1955**. A work first made public after its author's death is protected for 70 years from that date, and a song with no known author for 70 years from being made public. So for anonymous or traditional songs, find a printed source from before 1955 and take the words and tune from it, not from a later collection or transcription. The 2005 move to life plus 70 years applied only to works still protected at that point ([Australian Copyright Council fact sheet](https://www.artslaw.com.au/images/uploads/Duration%20of%20Copyright%20%28G023v16%29.pdf); [Southern Cross University](https://www.scu.edu.au/about/copyright/key-copyright-concepts/length-of-copyright/); [Smartcopying](https://smartcopying.edu.au/guidelines/copyright-basics/how-long-does-copyright-last/)).
- **US:** the song was **published in 1930 or earlier**, as of 1 January 2026 ([Duke Center for the Study of the Public Domain](https://web.law.duke.edu/cspd/publicdomainday/2026/)).

Both tests matter because Australia does not apply the "rule of the shorter term", so a song coming out of copyright in the US does not free it here ([Copyright expiration in Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_expiration_in_Australia)). All eight current songs pass, and their credit lines in `js/songs.js` already record why. The traps are well known:

- **Kookaburra and "Give Me a Home Among the Gumtrees":** Kookaburra's writer, Marion Sinclair, died in 1988, so it stays protected until about the end of 2058 ([Australian Dictionary of Biography](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/sinclair-marion-15924)). The gum-trees song dates from the 1970s.
- **Modern versions of traditional songs** can carry their own rights, and courts decide case by case whether a version adds enough. In 2025 Korea's Supreme Court found that one composer's 2011 Baby Shark did not differ enough from the traditional campfire chant to be protected ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2025/08/14/asia/baby-shark-copyright-court-pinkfong-intl-hnk)). An old printed source avoids the question.
- **Mama Lisa's World,** a popular source of children's songs, reserves all rights, including to its translations ([Mama Lisa's World](https://www.mamalisa.com/)).
- **Waltzing Matilda** should stay in its 1903 Marie Cowan form, since a 1941 US copyright registration of a later version is disputed ([Roger Clarke](http://www.rogerclarke.com/WM/Copyright.html)).

The national anthem can be used without permission for non-commercial purposes if its official words are left unaltered, though the government's page words this inconsistently and is worth reading directly ([PM&C](https://www.pmc.gov.au/honours-and-symbols/australian-national-symbols/australian-national-anthem/australian-national-anthem-use-and-protocol)). Good free sources exist. The Mutopia Project offers **2,124** public-domain or Creative Commons pieces with MIDI files ([Mutopia](https://www.mutopiaproject.org/)), and the Choral Public Domain Library offers MIDI and MusicXML (sheet-music file) downloads ([CPDL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choral_Public_Domain_Library)). Most free "children's music" on stock sites is instrumental background, not sing-along songs ([Chosic](https://www.chosic.com/free-music/creative-commons/)). A first batch to check song by song: bush songs such as "Click Go the Shears", "The Wild Colonial Boy" and "Botany Bay", plus rounds, old folk songs, and hymns and carols with pre-1955 authors. Creative Commons songs need care, because transposing a song and adding accompaniment adapts it. Use CC BY songs, like public-domain ones, freely with credit. Use CC BY-SA songs (Mutopia's own licence) only with a same-licence notice on that song's note data. Avoid "no derivatives" (ND) songs entirely, and use non-commercial (NC) songs only while the app stays free. A source and licence line for each song, plus a small "About these songs" page, keeps the paper trail.

Original songs are the cleanest way to grow the library, and the most motivating. Australian copyright needs a human author, so what the children write belongs to them, while wholly AI-generated material is not protected ([Bell Senior](https://bellsenior.com.au/resources/technology/ai-music-copyright-apra-amcos-australia/)). A tune Claude drafts with little human input is therefore free to use, as long as it doesn't accidentally echo a famous song. Songs written around a teaching goal (only do-re-mi, or a single octave leap) suit a pitch app better than pop songs, and children are proud of "our song". Ask the child before putting their song in the public repo, and credit them by first name or nickname only.

For the children's real favourites, a private **"Family songs" editor** is the practical answer. A parent types the words one syllable per note (the way the existing songs are stored). The melody goes in by tapping a keyboard, or later by singing it once Free sing can work out the notes in a sung tune (the next section). The song lives only on that phone. Importing a MusicXML file the family has bought is a cleaner way to get an accurate melody. MuseScore sells licensed scores individually, and its Pro subscription (about **US$6.99 a month**) unlocks downloads of many user-uploaded scores ([MuseScore forum](https://musescore.org/en/node/366450)). Strictly speaking, typing a pop song into a phone is still a copy with no clear Australian exception. Fair dealing for research or study is the only arguable fit, and it is judged case by case ([libcopyright.org.au](https://libcopyright.org.au/exceptions-to-copyright/)). But nothing is communicated to the public, and GitHub's takedown process applies only to what is in a repo ([GitHub](https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/content-removal-policies/dmca-takedown-policy)). The realistic risk for the parent is very low, much like handwriting lyrics for a child to practise. Part of the exposure is yours, though. The app is public, so any family in the world can use the editor. Under Australian law, the publisher of a tool can be said to "authorise" copying that others do with it. The design rules below are the reasonable steps that keep that risk low, and a question to Arts Law should include it. The editor should:

- have no upload, sync or public sharing, and leave Family songs out of every Share (a backup file the family keeps is fine);
- keep lyrics out of "Copy results for Claude";
- sit behind a parent PIN;
- say plainly: "Family songs are saved only on this phone and are never uploaded or shared. Only add songs for your own family's practice."

Never add a "search any song" feature that pulls lyrics from the web. That would make the app itself the copier.

For pop songs there is a middle path: a karaoke-video screen that a parent switches on. Embedding an official karaoke video is allowed if the player stays visible (at least 200 by 200 pixels) and its sound isn't separated out, and a children's site must declare itself child-directed ([YouTube API terms](https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/required-minimum-functionality); [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/171780?hl=en)). The costs are real:

- Since **10 December 2025**, Australians under 16 cannot sign in to YouTube, so account-based parental controls and content filters no longer reach them ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16758081?hl=en-GB)).
- The embedded player sets YouTube identifiers and shows Google's related videos, a far larger data flow than anything else in the app.
- The backing track leaks into the mic unless the child wears wired headphones.
- There is no lane of target notes, only the child's live pitch line.

| Option | Value | Cost | Effort | Privacy | Legal risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| More public-domain songs that pass both tests (folk, rounds, bush songs, carols), with source and licence lines | High | A$0 | Small per batch | None | Very low |
| Original family songs, written with the kids or drafted with Claude | High; tailored teaching and pride | A$0 | Small | Credit by first name only | Very low |
| On-phone "Family songs" editor (type the words, tap or sing the tune) | High; the kids' real favourites | A$0 | Medium | Good if it never uploads | Low in practice; grey for the parent, and partly for you as the tool's publisher |

| Formal licensing (Online Mini plus each publisher, or a lyric service) | Low for the cost | A$275+ a year plus unknown publisher and service fees | Large | Poor (lyric-service tracking) | Low once cleared, if a category fits at all |
| Copyrighted songs in the public repo or site | None | None | None | None | **Do not**: a takedown is the likely result |

Start with the paper trail (source and licence lines and an "About these songs" page), then add public-domain songs in small batches. Write the first family song together as a project. Build the Family songs editor with tap-in melodies first, add MusicXML import next, and add sing-to-enter once Free sing can work out the notes in a sung tune. Treat the karaoke-video screen as optional and last. Before anything in a grey area goes public, a question to the Arts Law Centre or APRA AMCOS costs little.

## Free sing should become a sing, hear, see and sing-again loop

**Recommendation: yes to recording and playback, and yes to "scoring" in the sense of writing the tune down as notes. No percentage grade for young children.** Today Free sing draws the live pitch line and keeps only about the last 10 seconds of pitch readings. It stores nothing and says nothing once the child stops (`js/app.js`). The upgrade is a loop: sing, hear it back with the line replayed, see the tune as coloured note blocks, sing it again, dress it up, and keep it on the phone. The header can change from "No score. Just your voice." to "Make up a song. Hear it back."

Made-up songs are a normal and important part of childhood, not a lesser version of "real" singing. Invented song appears at about **18 months and continues to about age 7**, and researchers treat it as "foundational in the development of creative thought and activity in music" ([Barrett 2006](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/43515220_Inventing_songs_inventing_worlds_The_'genesis'_of_creative_thought_and_activity_in_young_children's_lives)). Bjørkvold studied children's singing in three Oslo kindergartens. He described their "fluid" songs, full of glides, tiny pitch steps and free rhythm, as "quite different from what we adults traditionally identify as song" ([Bjørkvold, The Muse Within](https://archive.org/details/musewithincreati0000bjrk)). Susan Young argues that adult-led "performance" models of early music teaching have overlooked this spontaneous singing ([Young 2006](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2304/ciec.2006.7.3.270)). Control of pitch develops in a known order ([Gudmundsdottir et al., citing Gardner, Davidson & McKernon](https://opinvisindi.is/server/api/core/bitstreams/a18143f5-912b-4529-9274-ca19ada70eb1/content)):

- 2- and 3-year-olds reproduce the up-and-down shape of a phrase.
- 4-year-olds keep the steps of a scale within a phrase but drift into a new key between phrases.
- **Staying in one key appears no earlier than about 5 to 6.**
- Children under 5 often sing within no more than **seven semitones**.

A score for how in tune a 4-year-old's invented song was would mostly measure the child's age.

The evidence does support feedback that needs no "correct" answer.

- **Feedback works.** A meta-analysis of **34 studies** of singing instruction for 5- to 11-year-olds found an overall moderate benefit (an effect size of 0.43). **Feedback was the strongest teaching condition**, and 8-year-olds gained most ([meta-analysis](https://www.academia.edu/33946857/The_effects_of_instruction_on_the_singing_ability_of_children_ages_5_to_11_A_meta_analysis)).
- **Your own voice is the easiest model.** Singers copy **recordings of themselves more accurately than recordings of others**. Weaker singers gain most, and the advantage holds even when the recording is moved into a different key ([Pfordresher & Mantell 2014](http://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Mantell_2014_CogPsy.pdf)). This is the basis for the earlier review's "match yourself" idea ([earlier review, B4](Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review.md)), and it makes "sing your own tune again" the strongest learning hook in Free sing.
- **Praise the effort, not the child.** 5- and 6-year-olds praised for their effort and strategy kept going after a setback more than those praised as being good at it ([Kamins & Dweck 1999](https://people.uncw.edu/hungerforda/infancy/pdf/person%20versus%20process%20praise%20and%20criticism.pdf)).
- **Teachers' improvising games point the same way.** Orff and Kodály teachers start with sung question-and-answer games and use the five-note (pentatonic) scale, so children can "create beautiful music… without having to worry about making mistakes" ([Teaching With Orff](https://teachingwithorff.com/vocal-improvisation/); [SUNY, Music and the Child](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/suny-music-and-the-child/chapter/chapter-4-approaches-to-music-education-2/)).

The music tools children come back to share a handful of features, and all of them work without other people. Chrome Music Lab's Song Maker turns notes into a coloured grid that is "easy enough for a preschooler". It accepts sung notes and has a pentatonic mode, and South Australia's Department for Education recommends it for learning at home ([minibop music](https://minibopmusic.com/blog/what-is-chrome-music-lab); [SA Department for Education](https://www.education.sa.gov.au/our-learning-sa/learning-home-activities/arts/chrome-music-lab-song-maker)). Its Voice Spinner plays a recorded voice back at different speeds, and GarageBand's Chipmunk, Robot and Monster effects are "essentially Photo Booth for audio" ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/212224/garageband_ipad_04.html)). Blob Opera harmonises with whatever the user sings, live ([Experiments with Google](https://experiments.withgoogle.com/blob-opera)). Incredibox, rated 9+, hides animated bonuses behind particular sound combinations and lets users save mixes of about three minutes ([Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/website-reviews/incredibox); [Incredibox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incredibox)). Toca Band, rated 3+, has characters perform but no way to save ([Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/app-reviews/toca-band)). Yoto's Make Your Own cards let families load their own recordings for a child's audio player ([Look Hear Australia](https://lookhearaustralia.com.au/making-your-own-yoto-cards-fast-and-detailed-guide-%F0%9F%8E%B5%F0%9F%93%9A/)). Roughly, Toca Band and Chrome Music Lab suit 4–7-year-olds, Incredibox and Song Maker suit 8–12, and GarageBand-style polish suits teens. The social singing apps teens find are a different matter. BandLab requires users in Australia to be **at least 16** ([BandLab](https://help.bandlab.com/hc/en-us/articles/52223951629465-Minor-Safety)). Common Sense Media rates Smule 16+, and a parent-safety service advises against it for under-18s because of contact with strangers ([Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/app-reviews/smule-the-1-singing-app); [SaferKid](https://www.saferkid.com/app-reviews-for-parents/sing-karaoke-by-smule)). Note by Note should borrow the private features (instant playback, voice costumes, characters, note grids, hidden unlocks, a keepsake book) and replace the social ones with sharing inside the family.

The table below sets out feedback for each age band. It draws on the order of development above, the finding that children sing their own tunes back more easily, and readiness for part-singing at about 7 or 8 ([Sarrazin, Music and the Older Child](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Early_Childhood_Education/Music_and_the_Child_%28Sarrazin%29/01:_Chapters/1.09:_Music_and_the_Older_Child)). Each band keeps everything from the bands before it. Nothing in Free sing is ever marked red or "wrong". For older children, notes outside the detected key can be "adventure notes" rather than errors. The app does not know anyone's age today (`js/store.js` holds only range, settings, progress and streak days). So a parent should set an age band on each phone, which picks the feedback tier. Numbers stay off by default under 10.

A standard method (Krumhansl–Schmuckler) can find the key by comparing how long each note was sung with the patterns of all 24 major and minor keys ([music-key-finder](https://github.com/Corentin-Lcs/music-key-finder)). It was designed for composed music and has not been tested on children's invented songs. So when a take has fewer than about eight to ten clear notes, or is mostly glides, the app should say nothing about key. That threshold is a judgement, not a published figure. Likewise, no evidence turned up on whether 4- and 5-year-olds can deliberately repeat their own invented tune. Try "sing it again" with the youngest child first, and hide its stars for that age if they frustrate.

Recording works on iPhone, with one trap. The browser's standard recorder has an unresolved August 2025 report of **working once in a Home Screen app and then failing until the phone restarts** ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/797987)). The safer design copies sound straight from the audio engine the app already runs for pitch detection and saves it as an ordinary WAV audio file. That needs no extra library and keeps the recording exactly in step with the pitch line ([AudioWorklet support](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/AudioWorklet.json)). Memory is the next limit. On an iPhone SE (3rd generation) running iOS 26.2, pages have been reported to reload at about **100 MB** of memory ([Lapcat Software](https://lapcatsoftware.com/articles/2026/1/7.html); [pooled issue](https://github.com/Nehanth/pooled/issues/207)). A 3-minute take at the usual 48 kHz already fills about 35 MB. The studio in the next section would hold a tuned copy and a stereo mix alongside it, roughly 140 MB at full rate. So downsample takes to 22–24 kHz mono, free buffers between stages, and cap takes at roughly 60–90 seconds for under-8s and 3 minutes for older children. The caps are design defaults, not researched numbers.

Kept recordings belong in the app's own storage on the phone. Home Screen apps are exempt from Safari's rule that deletes a website's data after seven days without use. The app can also ask iOS to protect its storage, but iOS decides by its own rules ([WebKit tracking prevention](https://webkit.org/tracking-prevention/); [WebKit storage policy](https://webkit.org/blog/14403/updates-to-storage-policy/)). Developers in 2026 still treat an exported file as the only reliable backup ([Neurite issue](https://github.com/PXMYH/Neurite/issues/59)), so a "Save to Files" backup is essential. One hosting decision belongs before recordings exist. **Every GitHub Pages site under one account shares the same web address origin, and therefore the same storage** ([github-pages-local-storage](https://github.com/TomasHubelbauer/github-pages-local-storage)). Any other site published under lbecker17.github.io could read or delete the children's recordings. If you publish nothing else there and don't plan to, the risk is theoretical. Give the app's stored data distinctive names and skip the move. If you do publish other sites, move Note by Note before recordings build up, to its own GitHub organisation (free) or a custom domain (a yearly fee). The move has costs. Storage belongs to the old address, so each child loses their saved range, progress and streak unless the app first gains a progress export and import, which it lacks today. Every Home Screen icon must be removed and added again. And the old site should be replaced with a page pointing to the new one.

Sharing through the share sheet has worked since iOS 15 ([Bits and Pieces](https://blog.bitsrc.io/sharing-files-from-ios-15-safari-to-apps-using-web-share-c0e98f6a4971?gi=c8e0ed092d48)), but it cannot limit who receives a file. For under-13s, put Share, backup and the Family songs editor behind a parent PIN. A press-and-hold or a simple sum stops only the youngest, and teens can pass any gate. Keep press-and-hold for low-stakes settings. Next to Share, say that recordings must not be uploaded to AI music or voice apps (see the next section). Change the privacy line from "Nothing is recorded or sent anywhere" to "Your songs stay on this phone unless a grown-up shares them". Do it once the fonts are self-hosted, so the line is true (next section). Put Delete on every recording and Delete all in Settings. "Copy results for Claude" should carry only text, such as minutes sung and range, never audio. Finally, no safe daily amount of singing has been established ([NCVS](https://ncvs.org/strategies-for-safety-thresholds-of-phonation-for-performers-via-dosimetry/)), so a gentle "time to rest your voice" nudge after about 20 minutes is a sensible convention, not a rule.

Build Free sing from the note-finder outwards. Keep the whole take, work out its notes, and show "See my song", a feedback card and "play my tune" on piano, all without recording any audio. That builds the piece almost everything later depends on and changes nothing about privacy. Play the tune back as a song next (following section). Recording with voice costumes needs only the phone test, so it can start in parallel. Then come "sing it again", the My recordings book, question and answer, the harmony buddy and, last, the voice studio.

## Their own voice becomes a song on the phone, not in the cloud

**Recommendation: build a "Make it a song" feature inside the app that runs entirely on the phone, and do not connect any AI music service.** The on-phone version keeps the child's real voice, costs nothing and sends nothing anywhere. The cloud services that could re-sing a tune in a cloned copy of the child's voice are closed to young children by their own terms, and they cannot be connected safely to a public website.

The on-phone version is a chain of well-understood steps, done in two stages. The first stage works from the pitch line alone and needs **no audio recording at all**, which makes it the best starting point:

1. Turn the pitch line into notes.
2. Snap the notes to a beat the child picks (slow, medium or fast), rather than guessing a tempo from wandering singing.
3. Find the key.
4. Choose one simple chord per bar from the four most common (I, IV, V and vi).
5. Play the tune back with the app's existing piano sound plus a synthesized drum pattern.

The second stage, the voice studio, lays the child's actual recorded voice over that backing. Timing is the catch. Free sing plays no beat, so a child sings in free time. A backing snapped to a tempo chosen afterwards will not line up with the unsnapped voice. The studio must do one of two things. It can record against a count-in and a soft click heard through wired earbuds, so the click stays out of the recording. Or it can use a "rubato" mode, where chords change at the detected notes and phrase breaks instead of on a grid. Stretching each sung note onto the grid is possible but adds work and audible artefacts. Rubato mode also answers young children's wandering, out-of-key tunes, which defeat a chord-picker; whether children enjoy it is untested. The studio adds echo and room reverb using building blocks every browser already has. It can also apply gentle "magic tune" correction that nudges each note towards the nearest note in the key, with a strength slider for 10+ and teens; a hard snap gives the robotic effect GarageBand fans enjoy. The result is mixed into one audio file the child can play and a parent can share. Because the app knows exactly which notes it played, it can record the child singing alone and add a clean backing afterwards, so no piano bleeds into the recording. When a child does sing along to a backing, wired earbuds work far better than AirPods. AirPods drop to call-quality sound when the mic is on and have known stuttering bugs ([WebKit bug 221334](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=221334)). Since version 18.4, Safari reports its speaker delay, which helps line the two up ([MDN compatibility data](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/AudioContext.json)). This corrects the older notes behind the earlier review, which said Safari lacked it.

Licences matter because the code is public.

- **Use:** SoundTouchJS for the tuning step. It is actively maintained, can process audio faster than real time, has an add-on that keeps shifted voices sounding natural, and uses the Mozilla Public License 2.0, which fits a public repo ([SoundTouchJS](https://github.com/cutterbl/SoundTouchJS)). A smaller MIT-licensed library, audiojs/pitch-shift, also offers voice-oriented methods ([audiojs/pitch-shift](https://github.com/audiojs/pitch-shift)).
- **Avoid:** Rubber Band, which is GPL-licensed or paid ([Rubber Band](https://github.com/breakfastquay/rubberband)), and Superpowered, which may not be launched publicly without a negotiated licence ([Superpowered](https://superpowered.com/licensing)).
- **Hold back:** Spotify's Basic Pitch, a free (Apache-licensed) tool that turns audio into notes from a model of only about 0.9 MB ([basic-pitch-ts](https://github.com/spotify/basic-pitch-ts)). It pulls in the large TensorFlow.js library. In a 2024 comparison it scored poorly on humming: 0.268 on a 0-to-1 accuracy score (F1) when note start times were counted ([Dynamic HumTrans](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.05455)). Try the app's own pitch tracker first.

Full AI generation on the phone is out of reach. A browser-performance write-up calls the MusicGen text-to-music model "too heavy to load on arrival" in a web page ([SitePoint](https://www.sitepoint.com/webgpu-vs-webasm-transformers-js/)), and iPhone web pages reload under memory pressure at far smaller sizes, as the previous section showed.

Two free tools take this further without any upload. **GarageBand for iPhone** imports WAV and MIDI (note-data) files from the Files app. It adds a virtual Drummer and has an "Extreme Tuning" vocal effect that follows the song's key ([Apple: import](https://support.apple.com/guide/garageband-iphone/import-audio-and-midi-files-chsab9d208e/ios); [Apple: Drummer](https://support.apple.com/guide/garageband-iphone/use-drummer-chs9692b2133/ios); [Apple: Extreme Tuning](https://support.apple.com/en-us/101982)). A "Send to a grown-up" button can hand over a WAV of the voice and a MIDI file of the melody and chords. A parent or teen can then produce a full track while the child's real voice never leaves the family's devices. That screen, like Share, should carry the warning about AI services below.

**Claude** can write the words. The parent taps "Copy my tune", which copies the key, tempo, notes and phrase breaks as short text, and asks Claude for lyrics that fit, a chorus, chords and a title. The parent pastes the reply back. The app checks it and saves it to "Songs I made", the child's own tunes, which they can sing with words in the pitch lane. This extends the existing "Copy results for Claude" button, and only notes, never audio, leave the phone, by a parent's hand. It has to be a grown-up step: Claude.ai's consumer terms require users to be **18 or over** ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/protecting-well-being-of-users)). The parent should switch off the setting that lets Anthropic train on chats, since with it on Anthropic keeps them for **five years** instead of 30 days ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/updates-to-our-consumer-terms)). They should also leave the child's name and other identifying details out of the pasted text; a starter like "Sing about your pet" can invite them. AI models are also only moderately reliable with written music notation. They scored about **57%** on one 2025 benchmark of ABC-notation tasks (a text format for music) even with guidance ([arXiv 2503.22853](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.22853)). So the app's song format should be tiny and forgiving, with a preview before saving.

The cloud services are impressive and changing fast, but none suits a 4–12-year-old. Suno can take a hummed or sung clip and rebuild it with a full arrangement while keeping the melody and chords ([Suno handbook](https://suno.hk/suno-tutorial/suno-handbook-08-covers-audio/)). Its Voices feature launched in March 2026 and reached its phone apps in August 2026. It builds a model of the user's own singing voice after checking that the voice belongs to the account holder ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/suno-5-5-lets-users-sing-their-own-ai-generated-songs-with-a-personalized-voice-feature/); [Music Ally](https://musically.com/2026/08/10/suno-adds-vocal-recording-voices-feature-to-its-mobile-app/)).

- **Age:** Suno's terms, effective 3 September 2026, require users to be 18, or **13 to 17 with parental consent, and bar under-13s "in any capacity"** ([Suno Terms](https://suno.com/terms-of-service); [Suno Help](https://help.suno.com/en/articles/9720001)).
- **Data:** according to summaries of its privacy notice, Suno trains its models on what users submit, may treat a Voices model as biometric data, and keeps it **up to three years** after last use ([Suno Privacy](https://suno.com/privacy)).
- **Connecting it:** there is no official public way for apps to connect to it, only an "exploring" sign-up form posted on 1 July 2026 ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/suno-explores-developer-api-seeking-apps-that-unlock-experiences-generative-music-makes-possible-for-the-first-time/)).
- **Cost:** since September 2026 free accounts keep at most a small lifetime download allowance for personal, non-commercial use, and paid plans start at US$10 a month ([AllAboutAI](https://www.allaboutai.com/ai-news/free-suno-downloads-are-ending-as-warner-music-backs-licensed-ai-models/); [Lumi](https://lumimusic.ai/blog/suno-pricing)).

The law follows the same line as the technology. While Note by Note keeps everything on the phone, you, the owner, collect no personal information. The promise to the children needs two caveats. The app loads its two fonts from Google on every online launch (`index.html`), which sends each phone's IP address to Google. And GitHub logs the IP address of every visitor to a Pages site for security ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/pages/getting-started-with-github-pages/what-is-github-pages)). Self-hosting the fonts, which the earlier review already recommends ([Q10](Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review.md)), is a small job and should come before any reworded privacy line. Three points support the owner's position:

- The Privacy Act's principles do not apply to information an individual handles "only for the purposes of, or in connection with, his or her personal, family or household affairs" ([Privacy Act s 16](https://www.austlii.edu.au/cgi-bin/viewdoc/au/legis/cth/consol_act/pa1988108/s16.html)).
- Australia's coming **Children's Online Privacy Code** was released as a draft on 31 March 2026 and must be registered by **10 December 2026**. It applies to organisations already bound by the Privacy Act, and the small-business exemption (annual turnover of A$3 million or less) is expected to carry over ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/oaic-releases-exposure-draft-of-the-childrens-online-privacy-code); [Baker McKenzie](https://www.bakermckenzie.com/en/insight/publications/2026/05/australia-childrens-online-privacy-code-exposure-draft)).
- The Code's principles still make a good design checklist: parental consent for under-15s, collecting only what is strictly necessary, a right to deletion, and the child's best interests ([Attorney-General](https://ministers.ag.gov.au/media-centre/draft-childrens-online-privacy-code-released-31-03-2026)).

The picture changes the moment audio is uploaded. The OAIC (Australia's privacy regulator) lists voice among biometric features, and biometric information used to identify someone counts as sensitive information ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/your-privacy-rights/surveillance-and-monitoring/biometric-scanning)). Under the US children's privacy rule (COPPA), any recording of a child's voice is already personal information. Since the 2025 amendments (compliance from 22 April 2026), **voiceprints** and other biometric identifiers are as well ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule); [BBB National Programs](https://bbbprograms.org/media/insights/blog/coppa-amended)). An app that sent a child's song to Suno would hand a child's personal, and possibly biometric, data to a company whose terms forbid that child as a user. And a public website cannot hide the secret access keys these services require, so anyone could copy them from the code. The responsible boundary is an export a grown-up starts: the app makes the song on the phone and a grown-up taps Share. Only a 13–17-year-old using their own Suno account with a parent's consent fits Suno's terms, knowing that the voice model is kept and used for training. **A parent must not upload a child's recording to Suno, ElevenLabs, Kits.ai or similar through the parent's own account.** Suno's Voices and ElevenLabs' voice cloning require the account holder's own voice. For under-13s no AI service is appropriate, whoever presses send.

| Any AI service connected inside the app, or a parent uploading a child's voice on their own account | High "wow" | Large | Subscriptions | **High**: breaks the services' terms; biometric data; keys exposed in public code |

Build it in three stages. Stage 1 plays the tune back as a song from the pitch line alone, with chords, drums and a tempo choice. Stage 2 saves it to Songs I made, where the child can sing it again in the pitch lane, and adds the Claude lyrics round trip that a parent runs. Stage 3 records the child's voice against a count-in or in rubato mode. It mixes the voice with the backing, echo, reverb and optional tuning, and adds "Send to a grown-up" with WAV and MIDI files for GarageBand. Try Basic Pitch only if the app's own note-finding struggles with the youngest voices. Mention cloud services, if at all, only in a help note for parents of teens.

## One build order serves all four questions, starting with a phone check

Several pieces of work serve more than one question, so one sequence is more efficient than four. The most important shared piece is the **note-finder**, which turns a pitch line into notes. It powers Free sing's note blocks, feedback cards, "sing it again" and "make it a song", and it lets parents enter Family songs by singing. It is also the engine the earlier review's note-level scoring (N1) needs ([earlier review](Note%20by%20Note%20singing%20app%20review.md)). Building it once pays off four times. The second shared piece is the mic fix from the first section (mic off between screens, or a "narrating" flag). Besides narration, it keeps Free sing's prompts and "sing it again" playback off the pitch line. For planning, the research notes put recording, feedback cards and voice costumes together at about a week of work. They put "play my tune as a song" plus saving it at weeks rather than months.

Three things work this week with nothing built. Older children can record straight into GarageBand's Audio Recorder, where Extreme Tuning, the Drummer and the Chipmunk and Robot effects give a real song in their own voice now; a parent can do the recording for the younger ones. Whether GarageBand is available on a child's Apple Account by default was not checked. Chrome Music Lab's Song Maker, which takes sung notes, and its Voice Spinner both run in Safari with no account. For a pop song, play an official karaoke video on a second device through headphones while the child sings in Free sing.

Start building with a phone check on the children's iPhones, opened from the Home Screen. Several facts in this report come from developers' reports rather than Apple's documentation, and iOS changes often: an iOS 26.1 beta briefly broke microphone input in 2025 ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/802555)). Two checks work with today's app and take about ten minutes:

1. Does app sound leak onto the pitch line? Turn Headphones on in Settings without plugging any in, start a lesson at normal volume, stay silent through the singing part, and watch for a line.
2. Does the mic start reliably every time, including after the phone has been locked?

The second check matters because of a report from 29 September 2026. A developer found that switching echo cancellation off at the moment the mic opens, which is what Note by Note does, silenced the mic in an iPhone Home Screen app. Applying the setting just afterwards fixed it ([yamachat](https://github.com/yamboleeroy-ai/yamachat/pull/80)). The children use the app daily, so it presumably works on their phones today, but a fallback costs little. The remaining checks need a throwaway test page, because the app has no recording or file-sharing code yet. Claude builds the page, and you open it from the Home Screen on each phone for about 20 minutes. It checks whether recording still works on a second launch, whether the share sheet accepts a WAV file, and whether a 3-minute take, plus a full studio render of it, finishes on the oldest phone without the page reloading.

Five things stay off the list entirely:

- voice commands;
- any AI music or voice service connected inside the app;
- copyrighted songs in the public repo or on the site;
- lyric services and lyric scrapers;
- links that send children to social singing apps.

Each fails for the same reason: it moves a child's voice, or someone else's song, off the phone and into public or commercial hands.

## Conclusion

The four questions looked like separate feature requests, but they share one answer: design every feature so that **nothing leaves the phone unless a grown-up sends it**. That single rule removes three problems at once. Copyright exposure exists only on the public site and repo. Children's-privacy exposure begins only at upload. Age limits apply only to outside services. The rule also keeps the app's promise to the kids with a small, honest rewording, once the fonts come from the app itself rather than Google. It also exposes the one weak point in the hosting. The public GitHub site is where the copyright risk lives. And if other projects share its address, it is the least private place to keep children's recordings. That makes the hosting decision something to settle before any recordings arrive, not after.

The deeper point is that the children's own songs answer two questions at once. Legally, they are the only songs that are both unrestricted and personally meaningful. For learning, the research on copying your own voice means a child's own tune is an easier and fairer practice target than anyone else's. Free sing can fill a "Songs I made" book: each tune is played back as a song on the phone, given words with a parent's help through Claude, and sung again in the pitch lane. That turns invention into practice in a way no licensed catalogue could. The things to watch are dated: the Children's Online Privacy Code's registration by 10 December 2026, Suno's developer access and age rules, and whether recording in Home Screen apps becomes reliable. None of them changes the plan, because none of it depends on an outside service.
