# The science of singing pitch accuracy and real-time visual pitch feedback

Research notes, compiled 30 September 2026. Evidence labels used throughout:
- **[Empirical]** = peer-reviewed empirical study
- **[Review/theory]** = peer-reviewed review, theory or tutorial
- **[Pedagogy]** = established teaching tradition or teacher-practitioner writing
- **[Anecdote/marketing]** = blog, vendor or forum material

Method caveat: in this session, full-text fetching was blocked for almost every publisher domain (Frontiers, PMC, SAGE, ERIC, university servers). So most findings below come from search-engine abstracts and snippets of the named papers, not from reading the full texts. Numbers that could not be tied to a specific paper with confidence are marked. The report writer should treat effect-size figures as "reported in abstract/summary" rather than independently checked.

## 1. Why do people sing out of tune, how common is it, and what accuracy thresholds do researchers use?

### Takeaway
Most "tone-deaf" adults are not tone deaf. True perceptual amusia affects about 1.5% of people. Inaccurate singing (about 10 to 20% of adults, depending on the threshold and task) mostly comes from a weak *sensorimotor translation* between the pitch heard and the vocal gesture needed to make it. Memory and vocal-motor control add to this. It is largely trainable. The field's standard cut-offs are a 100-cent (one semitone) average error for "poor-pitch" (Pfordresher & Brown) and a stricter 50 cents (Dalla Bella & Berkowska). Accuracy (average error) and precision (consistency) are separate problems.

### Cited Findings
- [Empirical] Pfordresher & Brown (2007, *Music Perception*) proposed a "vocal pitch-matching deficit." Poor-pitch singers could discriminate pitch as well as accurate singers, so the problem is a mis-mapping of perceived pitch onto vocal action (sensorimotor translation), not perception, motor or memory alone. When imitating unfamiliar 4-note sequences, about 10 to 15% of participants were classed as poor-pitch singers (errors of at least one semitone). — [Pfordresher & Brown 2007 PDF](http://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf); summary via [JRME/Pfordresher & Demorest 2021](https://ubwp.buffalo.edu/apal-pfordresher/wp-content/uploads/sites/79/2022/01/PfordresherDemorest_2021_JRME.pdf)
- [Empirical] Poor-pitch singers mainly show a consistent overall distortion of absolute pitch (for example, always compressed toward a comfortable note). They also show a restricted range when matching pitch, which their physical vocal range does not explain. — [Pfordresher & Mantell 2014, *Cognitive Psychology*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010028514000036); [Pfordresher & Greenspon 2025, *Musicae Scientiae*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10298649241289542)
- [Empirical] "Singing with yourself": poor-pitch singers imitate recordings of *their own* voice better than recordings of other people. This supports a deficient "inverse model" (the internal mapping from a target sound to the motor command) rather than a perceptual deficit. — [Pfordresher & Mantell 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010028514000036) ([PDF](http://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Mantell_2014_CogPsy.pdf))
- [Empirical] Hutchins & Peretz (2012, "A frog in your throat or in your ear?"): poor vocal imitators could match pitch accurately with a manual slider, so their pitch discrimination was intact. They were more accurate when matching their own recorded voice, which points to vocal-motor translation. — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51603187_A_Frog_in_Your_Throat_or_in_Your_Ear_Searching_for_the_Causes_of_Poor_Singing); summary in [Frontiers in Human Neuroscience 2015 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00271/full)
- [Empirical] Pfordresher et al. (2015, *Attention, Perception & Psychophysics*): "Singing ability is rooted in vocal-motor control of pitch" (title claim). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-014-0732-1)
- [Empirical] Dalla Bella and Berkowska: about 85 to 90% of occasional (untrained) singers sing in tune, and about 10 to 15% are inaccurate when singing well-known melodies from memory or imitating pitch sequences. Memory is one source of poor singing, because load on melody memory degrades accuracy. The Sung Performance Battery found distinct "phenotypes" of poor singing (some people fail only from memory, some only in imitation). — [Dalla Bella 2009, *Annals NYAS*](https://nyaspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1749-6632.2009.04558.x); [Dalla Bella 2012 memory and vocal performance](https://nyaspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1749-6632.2011.06424.x); [Berkowska & Dalla Bella 2013, Sung Performance Battery, *Frontiers*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00714/full)
- [Empirical] Congenital amusia, a true pitch-perception disorder, is estimated at about 1.5% of the population (Peretz & Vuvan 2017). That is much rarer than inaccurate singing. — cited in [Pfordresher & Demorest 2021 (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343166548_The_Prevalence_and_Correlates_of_Accurate_Singing)
- [Empirical/threshold] Common cut-offs are: a mean absolute error greater than 100 cents (one semitone) defines "poor-pitch singers," with about 10 to 20% of people exceeding it (Pfordresher/Brown lab); and mean signed deviation greater than 50 cents (Berkowska & Dalla Bella 2009), which is stricter. — [Pfordresher & Mantell 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010028514000036); [Pfordresher & Demorest 2021](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343166548_The_Prevalence_and_Correlates_of_Accurate_Singing)
- [Review] Dalla Bella (2015, *Music Perception*) argues that prevalence estimates depend heavily on the measure and threshold used. Poor singing is a "problem of measurement and sensitivity," so no single prevalence figure exists. — [Dalla Bella 2015 PDF](https://dallabella-lab.ca/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Dalla-Bella_2015_Defining-Poor-Pitch-Singing-A-Problem-of-Measurement-and-Sensitivity.pdf)
- [Empirical] Pfordresher, Brown, Meier, Belyk & Liotti (2010, *JASA*, "Imprecise singing is widespread") separated accuracy (mean error) from precision (consistency across repeated attempts). Adult singing is usually accurate but not necessarily precise, and poor precision is more common than poor accuracy. — [JASA](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article/128/4/2182/683506/Imprecise-singing-is-widespread); [PDF](http://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher_etal_2010_JASA.pdf)
- [Empirical] Hutchins, Roquet & Peretz (2012, *Music Perception*), "The vocal generosity effect": listeners tolerate more mistuning in sung tones than in instrumental tones before judging them out of tune. — [Peretz lab PDF](https://peretzlab.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/hutchins_musicperception_2012.pdf) (the exact cents tolerances were not retrieved)
- [Review] Pfordresher (2022, *Annals NYAS*), "Singing accuracy across the lifespan," treats singing accuracy as a developmental, learned skill. — [Wiley](https://nyaspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/nyas.14815)
- **Superseded view:** the folk idea that inaccurate singers are "tone deaf" (a perceptual deficit) is not supported for most poor singers. The evidence above consistently dissociates poor singing from pitch perception. — [Pfordresher & Brown 2007](http://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf); [Hutchins & Peretz 2012](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51603187_A_Frog_in_Your_Throat_or_in_Your_Ear_Searching_for_the_Causes_of_Poor_Singing)

### Inferences
- A typical adult beginner using the app is very likely able to hear the difference and able to improve. The main target is the ear-to-voice mapping, so practice should be imitation-heavy (hear, then produce) across many pitches.
- The app's "average miss in cents" measures accuracy. It should also report *consistency* (the spread across repeats), because imprecision is the more common problem (Pfordresher et al. 2010).
- A one-size threshold is arbitrary. The 50- and 100-cent research cut-offs are for *classifying people*, not for judging single notes. A beginner-friendly per-note "in tune" band is probably somewhere around ±25 to 50 cents, tightening over time. This is an inference; no study was found that validates a specific per-note band for training.
- Singing from memory (songs) and imitating a heard model (exercises) are partly separate skills (Dalla Bella's phenotypes). An app that only ever sings *with* a guide never trains the memory route.

### Gaps
- Exact prevalence figures and sample sizes from Pfordresher & Demorest (2021, JRME), including by task (single pitch vs interval vs song), could not be retrieved because the full text was blocked.
- Typical mean error in cents for untrained adults on each task was not retrieved as specific numbers.
- The specific tolerance values (in cents) for the vocal generosity effect were not retrieved.

## 2. What does research show about real-time visual feedback for singing pitch?

### Takeaway
Real-time pitch displays reliably improve accuracy *while the display is on*, and they help beginners more than skilled singers. The few studies that test learning without the display are mixed. The best-controlled adult study (Paney & Tharp 2021, 10 weeks) found that both groups improved but the visual-feedback group was not significantly better. Berglin, Pfordresher & Demorest (2022) found visual feedback helped poor-pitch adults mostly on 4-note melodies, not single notes. Evidence is small-sample, short-term and rarely tests retention or transfer. The 2026 systematic review flags this as the field's main weakness.

### Cited Findings
- [Review] Real-time visual feedback for singing was first proposed and studied by Graham Welch and colleagues. The display removes the time delay of verbal feedback from a teacher. — [*Frontiers in Psychology* 2021, "Effects of Visual and Auditory Feedback in Violin and Singing Voice Pitch Matching Tasks"](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.684693/full) (authors not confirmed in retrieved text)
- [Empirical, older] Welch (1985): 66 children aged 7 to 9 practised pitch matching. They either repeated the single target pitch (low variability) or practised 6 *other* pitches (high variability). They received real-time visual feedback with a target reference line, feedback without a reference, or no visual feedback. Feedback had a significant overall effect. Within the no-feedback group, variable practice beat fixed practice, consistent with schema theory. — [Summary in Frontiers in Human Neuroscience 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2014.00598/full); [Welch 1985 schema theory, *Psychology of Music*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735685131001)
- [Empirical, older] Welch's group later built the SINGAD microcomputer system for singing assessment and development. A search summary reported "23% more accurate" pitch in 7-year-olds versus traditional teaching, but I could not confirm which paper this figure comes from. — [Microcomputer-based singing ability assessment and development (academia.edu)](https://www.academia.edu/73924969/Microcomputer_based_singing_ability_assessment_and_development)
- [Empirical] Wilson, Thorpe & Callaghan (2005), using the Australian Sing&See display: beginners benefited from the rich contextual display, but *skilled* singers' pitch accuracy got *worse* with it, which the authors attributed to extra cognitive load. — [Looking at singing (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/239921722_Looking_at_singing_Does_real-time_visual_feedback_improve_the_way_we_learn_to_sing); [PDF mirror](https://www.afpc-evta-france.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/10/2015/02/72-feeback-visuel-et-apprentissage-chant.pdf)
- [Empirical] Wilson, Lee, Callaghan & Thorpe (2008, *Journal of Interdisciplinary Music Studies* 2(1&2): 157–172) compared a detailed "Grid" pitch display (Sing&See prototype) with a simpler "Keyboard" display that marks only the current note. A search summary reported a larger performance decrement for Grid users than Keyboard users (7.7 Hz vs 4.7 Hz), consistent with display complexity adding cognitive load. The attribution of these numbers to this paper is likely but not confirmed from full text. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/251823345_Learning_to_sing_in_tune_Does_real-time_visual_feedback_help); [author PDF](https://patwilson.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2012/11/CIM07WilsonLearnSingTune.pdf)
- [Empirical] Paney & Kay (2015, *Update*): third-grade children used "SingingCoach," a game that shows the melody contour and a live line of the child's voice. Pre-to-post scores rose more than 10 percentage points (correlated-samples t-test). There was no control group in that summary. — [ERIC EJ1078711](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1078711); related: [Paney 2015 "Singing video games may help improve pitch-matching accuracy"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/273179190_Singing_video_games_may_help_improve_pitch-matching_accuracy)
- [Empirical, key adult RCT] Paney & Tharp (2021, *Psychology of Music* 49: 360–370): adult university non-music-majors were randomly assigned to SingingCoach with concurrent visual feedback or to a control, for 10 weeks. **Both groups improved significantly, but the feedback group did not score significantly higher at post-test.** Self-ratings of singing rose significantly. One secondary summary adds that the feedback group did better *during* training but decayed to control-group level when feedback was removed. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334113826_The_effect_of_concurrent_visual_feedback_on_adult_singing_accuracy)
- [Empirical] Berglin, Pfordresher & Demorest (2022, *Psychology of Music*) trained adult poor-pitch singers with visual feedback (the Erol Singing Studio app), auditory feedback, or neither. Training effects were significant only in the visual-feedback condition. That gain was specific to imitating **4-note melodies**, not to matching single pitches (piano or vocal timbre). The authors suggest visual feedback may substitute for faulty auditory-motor associations. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/03057356211026730); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/352064762_The_Effect_of_Visual_and_Auditory_Feedback_on_Adult_Poor-Pitch_Remediation)
- [Empirical] Frontiers in Psychology (2021), visual and auditory feedback in violin and singing pitch matching: experimental groups trained with visual feedback improved pitch accuracy significantly, while the control group showed no change. — [Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.684693/full)
- [Empirical] Leong & Cheng (2014, *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning*) studied real-time visual feedback with pre-service teachers' singing. A search summary mentioned pitch error reductions "up to 15 Hz" baseline-to-post-test, but I could not confirm which study that figure belongs to. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jcal.12046); [ERIC EJ1028987](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1028987)
- [Review, current] Zhang (2026, *Frontiers in Psychology*) systematically reviewed 36 studies of acoustic and spectral feedback (pitch traces, spectrograms, formant displays, biofeedback) in vocal learning. The review's central claim is that concurrent feedback raises performance during acquisition but risks "guidance dependency" once removed, while terminal feedback produces more stable retention. 29 of 36 studies did not formally test the feedback→mechanism→skill pathway, which the review calls the field's main methodological gap. — [Frontiers 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1920074/full); [PubMed 42694130](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42694130/)
- [Review] Other reviews of the area: "Real-Time Visual Feedback in Singing Pedagogy: Current Trends and Future Directions" and "Development of real-time visual feedback assistance in singing training: a review" (Hoppe, Sadakata & Desain 2006). — [ResearchGate](https://researchgate.net/publication/364726815_Real-Time_Visual_Feedback_in_Singing_Pedagogy_Current_Trends_and_Future_Directions); [Hoppe et al. review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220663218_Development_of_real-time_visual_feedback_assistance_in_singing_training_A_review)
- [Anecdote/marketing] Sing&See's vendor site hosts a "research" page summarising the Australian studies. It is useful as a pointer, but it is vendor material. — [Sing & See research page](https://www.singandsee.com/research-visual-feedback)
- **Older finding now qualified:** the early (1980s to 2000s) conclusion that "real-time visual feedback improves singing accuracy" came mostly from measuring performance *with* the display, often in children, with short follow-up. Paney & Tharp (2021) and the Zhang (2026) review qualify it: benefits during feedback do not reliably survive once the display is removed. — [Paney & Tharp 2021](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334113826_The_effect_of_concurrent_visual_feedback_on_adult_singing_accuracy); [Zhang 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42694130/)

### Inferences
- The pitch lane is a good *scaffold*, especially for beginners and for multi-note patterns (Berglin et al. 2022). The evidence does not support assuming that high scores with the lane on mean the user can sing in tune without the app.
- Simpler displays are likely better. A single target line plus the voice trace (Keyboard-like) should beat a busy grid, and extra visual elements (lyrics plus notes plus trace plus score) increase load.
- The app should measure what matters: performance on trials *without* the live trace.

### Gaps
- No retrieved study directly tested a phone-based scrolling pitch lane with a delayed, no-feedback retention test in self-guided adults.
- Effect sizes (Cohen's d) and sample sizes for Berglin et al. 2022, Paney & Tharp 2021 and the Frontiers 2021 study were not retrievable; full texts were blocked.
- The results of Pfordresher & Greenspon (2025), wide (octave) versus narrow (fifth) training range, were not retrieved. Only the design and hypothesis were: that wider-range training helps remediation.

## 3. Motor-learning feedback principles applied to singing: what schedule should an app use?

### Takeaway
Motor-learning research (the guidance hypothesis, feedback-frequency studies, and the speech-motor tutorial by Maas et al. 2008) converges on one design rule. Give frequent, specific, knowledge-of-performance feedback *early*. Then reduce it: fade it, use bandwidth (only flag errors outside a tolerance), and make it more terminal (after the attempt) to build retention and transfer. Direct attention externally, toward the sound or its effect, rather than to body parts. Singing-specific tests of these schedules are scarce; most support is extrapolated from general and speech motor learning.

### Cited Findings
- [Review/theory] The guidance hypothesis (Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter tradition) says concurrent feedback boosts acquisition performance because it continuously corrects errors. Learners then substitute the external signal for building an internal sensorimotor model, so performance often drops when feedback is removed. Terminal feedback gives more stable retention. — [Zhang 2026 systematic review, *Frontiers in Psychology*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1920074/full)
- [Review/theory] Maas et al. (2008, *AJSLP*), "Principles of motor learning in treatment of motor speech disorders," includes these principles:
  - Reducing feedback frequency or delaying it promotes retention and transfer.
  - Immediate or frequent feedback gives temporary performance gains but interferes with retention.
  - Knowledge-of-performance (KP) feedback may help most early on, or when learners cannot yet tell correct from incorrect attempts.
  - High-frequency feedback is best at the earliest stage; low-frequency feedback is best for long-term retention and works best with variable practice.

  — [ASHA](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1058-0360(2008/025)); [tutorial PDF](https://gwulf.faculty.unlv.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/05/Maas-et-al_AJSLP-2008_PML-tutorial.pdf); [20Q summary](https://www.speechpathology.com/articles/20q-principles-motor-learning-and-20589)
- [Empirical, related voice domain] Ambulatory voice biofeedback (*JSLHR*, 2016) tested relative-frequency and summary feedback schedules on performance and retention of a vocal behaviour in daily life. This shows feedback-schedule research exists for voice, although not for pitch. — [ASHA JSLHR](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2016_JSLHR-S-16-0164) (results not retrieved)
- [Empirical] Welch (1985) found that variable practice (practising pitches other than the test pitch) beat repeating the target pitch when no visual feedback was given. This fits schema theory's prediction that variability builds a generalisable rule. — [summary, Frontiers Hum Neurosci 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2014.00598/full); [Welch 1985](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735685131001)
- [Pedagogy/review] "The Application of Motor Learning Feedback in the Singing Voice Studio" argues that voice teachers often give excessive concurrent feedback, which hinders motor learning, and that the timing, quantity and content of feedback are crucial. — [academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/46864666/The_Application_of_Motor_Learning_Feedback_in_the_Singing_Voice_Studio)
- [Empirical] Focus of attention in singing: Atkins & Duke (2013, amateur singers, 3-note pattern) found that expert-rated tone quality was better with external foci (sending sound to fingertips by the nose, to a microphone, or to a point on a wall about 5 m away) than with an internal focus (feeling throat vibrations). — [Atkins & Duke, IJRCS PDF](https://acda-publications.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/IJRCS/volumefour/ijrcs4_2_atkinsduke.pdf)
- [Empirical] Atkins (2017, *JRME*, trained singers) found that distal external foci (further from the body) beat proximal or internal foci. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0022429416673842)
- [Review] Wulf & Mornell (2018) argue that an external focus enhances musical performance. A 2024 systematic review of focus of attention in music, and a 2021 NATS *Journal of Singing* review for singers, cover the area. The outcome measures in these studies are mostly tone quality, not pitch accuracy. — [Mornell & Wulf 2018 PDF](https://gwulf.faculty.unlv.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Mornell_Wulf_AF_music_2018.pdf); [Frontiers 2024 systematic review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1290596/full); [NATS JOS 2021](https://www.nats.org/_Library/JOS_On_Point/JOS_077_03_2021_407.pdf)
- [Empirical] Paney & Tharp (2021): 10 weeks of *concurrent* visual feedback gave no significant advantage at post-test over the control. This is consistent with the guidance hypothesis operating in singing. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334113826_The_effect_of_concurrent_visual_feedback_on_adult_singing_accuracy)

### Inferences
- Suggested schedule for an app (synthesis; no singing study has tested this exact schedule):
  1. **Early lessons:** show the concurrent pitch lane on every trial as KP feedback. It shows *how* the voice moved relative to the target, which matters because beginners cannot yet hear their own error.
  2. **Once a user passes a lesson:** switch some trials to "trace hidden while singing, replay after." This is terminal feedback, and the replay shows the trace over the targets.
  3. **Bandwidth:** only colour or flag notes outside a tolerance, and tighten it over levels (for example ±50 → ±35 → ±25 cents).
  4. **Summary feedback:** after a block of reps, show one summary instead of per-note judgements.
  5. **Self-controlled feedback:** let the user tap to reveal the trace. This comes from the general motor-learning literature (Wulf and colleagues); no singing-specific study was retrieved.
- Knowledge of results (a score or cents) and knowledge of performance (the trace shape: scooping, drifting flat on long holds) should both be present. KP should dominate early and KR should dominate later.
- Coaching text should use external or distal cues ("aim the sound at the far wall", "draw the line through the target") rather than anatomical cues ("lift your soft palate", "feel your larynx"). Most of this evidence concerns tone rather than pitch.

### Gaps
- No retrieved study directly compared concurrent, faded, bandwidth and terminal pitch feedback *for singing* with a delayed retention test.
- No singing-specific study on self-controlled feedback was retrieved.
- The results of the 2016 voice biofeedback schedule study were not retrieved.

## 4. The reference tone: unison vs echo, timbre of the model, octave confusion, drones, headphones vs speakers

### Takeaway
Model timbre matters. People match voices closest to their own best: their own voice first, then a same-sex or same-range human voice, then instruments or synthesized tones. Poor-pitch adults may be *hindered* by singing in unison with the correct pitch, while children in class may benefit. Imitating after the model (echo) is the core diagnostic and training task in the research. Masking your own voice (loud headphones, noise) reduces accuracy, while mild ear occlusion may help self-monitoring. Evidence on drones and on octave confusion in adult men is thin in what I retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- [Empirical] Hutchins & Peretz (2012): singers match their own recorded voice better than another voice or a synthetic target. The advantage grows as the target timbre approaches the singer's own, suggesting similar vocal cues trigger the motor command more effectively. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51603187_A_Frog_in_Your_Throat_or_in_Your_Ear_Searching_for_the_Causes_of_Poor_Singing); [Frontiers Hum Neurosci 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00271/full)
- [Empirical] Pfordresher & Mantell (2014) found a self-imitation advantage in poor-pitch singers. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010028514000036)
- [Empirical] Watts & Hall (2008, *Logopedics Phoniatrics Vocology*), "Timbral influences on vocal pitch-matching accuracy": female participants matched female-voice targets more accurately than instrumental timbres, which the authors attributed to spectral similarity to their own voice. — [Taylor & Francis DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14015430802028434); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5286228_Timbral_influences_on_vocal_pitch-matching_accuracy)
- [Empirical] Recorded human voices matched to participants by gender and range elicited more accurate singing than synthesized complex tones. This finding appears in the pitch-matching literature summarised in Nichols (2020, *Psychology of Music*); the attribution to a specific primary study was not confirmed. — [Nichols 2020 PDF](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.psu.edu/dist/6/96535/files/2020/07/Nichols-2020-PoM-1.pdf)
- [Empirical] "Accuracy of Pitch Matching Significantly Improved by Live Voice Model" (*Journal of Voice*, 2013) reports, as its title states, that a live voice model beats other model types. The details were not retrieved. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199713000027)
- [Empirical] "The Effect of Timbre, Pitch, and Vibrato on Vocal Pitch-Matching Accuracy" (*Journal of Voice*, 2015/16) examined how target vibrato and timbre affect matching. The specific results were not retrieved. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199715001071)
- [Empirical] Berglin et al. (2022) included both piano-timbre and vocal-timbre single-pitch targets. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/03057356211026730)
- [Empirical] Unison vs solo and echo:
  - Poor-pitch singers "appeared to be hindered rather than helped by singing with correct accompaniment" (Pfordresher & Brown 2007 line of work), consistent with a pitch-to-action mapping problem. — [Pfordresher & Brown 2007](http://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf) (via search summary)
  - Green (1994, *JRME*): elementary children at each grade sang more accurately in unison with peers than individually. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.2307/3345495)
  - The broader literature is inconclusive: some studies favour doubled or unison singing, some favour solo, and some find no difference. — [Nichols 2016 JRME PDF](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.psu.edu/dist/6/96535/files/2018/12/Nichols-2016-1-1qxqwka.pdf)
  - In congenital amusia, singing by imitation helped in both unison and echo conditions, although pitch remained poor. — [Dalla Bella papers (academia.edu)](https://independent.academia.edu/SimoneDallaBella); [Singing in congenital amusia](https://www.academia.edu/12442981/Singing_in_congenital_amusia)
  - For unaccompanied unison and duet singing, pitch accuracy and interaction between singers have been measured directly. — [Singing together (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330860160_Singing_together_Pitch_accuracy_and_interaction_in_unaccompanied_unison_and_duet_singing)
- [Empirical] Estis & Moore (*Journal of Voice*, 2009/10) tested pitch matching under musical interference and noise. Trained singers outperformed untrained people in all conditions. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199709001891)
- [Empirical] Hearing yourself:
  - Pitch accuracy decreased when auditory feedback was masked by noise through headphones. — [Bottalico et al., "Effect of Training and Level of External Auditory Feedback on the Singing Voice: Pitch Inaccuracy," *J Voice*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5010534/)
  - After 3 years of professional voice training, singers showed a smaller masked-versus-unmasked difference, i.e. more reliance on internal (kinesthetic) feedback. — same source, citing the Mürbe et al. line of work
  - Some ear occlusion (for example headphones, covering an ear) has been associated with *higher* pitch-matching accuracy, and choirs and studios commonly cover an ear to hear oneself better. — ["An Investigation in the Measurable Differences between Pitch Perception in the Voice and Pitch Perception of External Sound Sources," *J Voice* 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0892199722003782)
- [Anecdote] Octave confusion: choir leaders report that when a woman sings a note for a man, he will usually sing it an octave lower, and they suggest strategies for cross-sex pitching. — [Chris Rowbury choir blog, 2016](https://blog.chrisrowbury.com/2016/05/how-male-singers-can-successfully-pitch.html)

### Inferences
- The app's echo (call-and-response) mode is not a lesser fallback for people without headphones. It is the standard research paradigm for building the ear-to-voice mapping, and poor-pitch adults may do *worse* singing along with the target. Singing with the guide tone should be treated as scaffolding to withdraw, not the default.
- Guide tones should be voice-like and in the singer's own octave or register, which the range test makes possible. A pure piano or sine tone at a different octave from the singer, for example a man hearing a female-register model, is the weakest model. Playing the user's own earlier good attempt back as the model is the strongest option in the evidence. It could be done in memory only, without saving a recording.
- With headphones, keep the guide quiet enough that the singer still hears their own voice (masking hurts). Consider suggesting one earbud out, which is mild occlusion plus own-voice hearing.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed adult study on octave confusion (men matching female or child models) was retrieved in this session. The older children's literature on male falsetto vs chest models is known to exist but was not retrieved.
- No study on drones for adult pitch accuracy was retrieved.
- The specific results of the live-voice-model and vibrato studies were not retrieved.

## 5. Recording and listening back: does self-listening improve pitch?

### Takeaway
Listening back to a recording alone gives little benefit. Self-listening combined with a model and structured self-evaluation improves accuracy, and it does so more than any single element. Audio playback makes self-assessment of sound more accurate. Evidence comes mostly from children and music students, not adult solo beginners.

### Cited Findings
- [Empirical] Children who did self-listening *plus* model-listening improved in melodic singing accuracy. Those who only listened to their own recording did not improve, and controls got worse at post-test. — [Self-evaluation, self-recording and self-listening study, Florida Music Educators' *Research Perspectives in Music Education* 2022](https://www.ingentaconnect.com/contentone/fmea/rpme/2022/00000022/00000001/art00003) (the exact attribution of this finding to this paper came from a search summary)
- [Empirical] Scaffolded model listening, playback and self-evaluation each produced greater accuracy gains than a control, and the combination produced the largest gains (Hewitt-style design, junior high instrumentalists). — [Effects of modeling, self-evaluation and self-listening (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249808922_The_Effects_of_Modeling_Self-Evaluation_and_Self-Listening_on_Junior_High_Instrumentalist'_Music_Performance_and_Practice_Attitude)
- [Empirical] Summitt & Fisher: 26 undergraduate musicians. The experimental group listened to playback of their own performance before self-evaluating and the control group did not. The study tested whether this made self-evaluation more accurate. — [Bulletin of the Council for Research in Music Education](https://scholarlypublishingcollective.org/uip/bcrme/article-abstract/doi/10.5406/bulcouresmusedu.209.0063/254508/Effect-of-Self-Listening-on-Self-Evaluation?redirectedFrom=fulltext); [Memphis repository](https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/facpubs/68/)
- [Empirical] Audio and video playback both improve the accuracy of music students' self-assessment; audio is slightly more accurate for auditory elements. — [Springer 2025 chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-95-1525-7_21)
- [Empirical, recent] AI-assisted feedback and reflection in vocal training has been studied for effects on metacognition and singing performance. Details were not retrieved; the study is relevant to the "Copy results for Claude" feature. — [Frontiers 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1598867/full)
- [Pedagogy] Recording and self-review is standard studio practice and part of self-regulated learning models. Hearing one's own performance supports self-regulated learning when paired with self-evaluation. — [summary in search results; Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-95-1525-7_21)

### Inferences
- A listen-back feature would be most useful as "model, then your take, then compare": play the target, then the user's attempt, then ask the user to judge (sharp, flat, or on) before revealing the app's verdict. That self-estimation step trains error detection, which matters once the app is gone.
- The app's privacy stance (nothing recorded) can be kept by holding the take in memory for immediate playback only and never storing it.

### Gaps
- No retrieved study examined adults self-teaching singing with record and playback.
- The results of Summitt & Fisher were not retrieved in detail.

## Implications for a self-guided singing app

### Takeaway
Keep the pitch lane but treat it as training wheels. Add no-feedback trials, faded and terminal feedback, echo-first practice, voice-like guides in the singer's register, consistency metrics, and "guess, then reveal" self-evaluation. Together these target transfer to singing without the app, which is the real goal and the thing the current design does not measure.

### Cited Findings (each implication, with the evidence it rests on)
1. **Add "lane-off" trials and score them separately.** In each lesson, after the user succeeds with the lane, repeat the item with the live trace hidden, then show the trace as a replay (terminal KP). Make the lane-off score the "real" progress metric. Evidence: [guidance hypothesis, Zhang 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42694130/); [Paney & Tharp 2021, no advantage of concurrent feedback at post-test](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334113826_The_effect_of_concurrent_visual_feedback_on_adult_singing_accuracy); [Maas et al. 2008](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1058-0360(2008/025)).
2. **Fade concurrent feedback with progress.** For example, 100% of trials with the lane at first, then 50%, then about 25% and summary-only. Evidence: [Maas et al. 2008 on reduced frequency for retention](https://gwulf.faculty.unlv.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/05/Maas-et-al_AJSLP-2008_PML-tutorial.pdf).
3. **Bandwidth feedback.** Only mark notes outside a tolerance (for example ±50 cents for beginners, tightening to ±25), rather than colouring every deviation. Evidence: [Berkowska & Dalla Bella 50-cent criterion](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343166548_The_Prevalence_and_Correlates_of_Accurate_Singing); [vocal generosity effect](https://peretzlab.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/hutchins_musicperception_2012.pdf); [Maas et al. 2008](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1058-0360(2008/025)). The specific band is an inference.
4. **Keep the display minimal**, especially during songs: a target line and the voice trace, with lyrics kept small. Offer a "focus" mode. Evidence: [Wilson et al. 2005, skilled singers worsened from display load](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/239921722_Looking_at_singing_Does_real-time_visual_feedback_improve_the_way_we_learn_to_sing); [Wilson et al. 2008, Grid vs Keyboard display](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/251823345_Learning_to_sing_in_tune_Does_real-time_visual_feedback_help).
5. **Make echo (hear, then sing) the primary mode even with headphones.** Treat singing with the guide as optional scaffolding the user can turn off, and prompt them to turn it off as they improve. Evidence: [poor-pitch singers hindered by correct accompaniment, Pfordresher & Brown 2007](http://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf); [echo imitation is the standard remediation paradigm, Berglin et al. 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/03057356211026730).
6. **Use a voice-like guide in the singer's own octave**, which the range test enables. Consider offering "sung 'ah' (male or female)" as a guide timbre alongside piano. Evidence: [Watts & Hall 2008](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5286228_Timbral_influences_on_vocal_pitch-matching_accuracy); [gender- and range-matched voices beat synth tones (Nichols 2020 summary)](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.psu.edu/dist/6/96535/files/2020/07/Nichols-2020-PoM-1.pdf); [Hutchins & Peretz 2012](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51603187_A_Frog_in_Your_Throat_or_in_Your_Ear_Searching_for_the_Causes_of_Poor_Singing).
7. **"Match yourself" mode.** Capture the user's best in-tune attempt in memory (never saved) and replay it as the next target. Evidence: [self-imitation advantage, Pfordresher & Mantell 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010028514000036); [Hutchins & Peretz 2012](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51603187_A_Frog_in_Your_Throat_or_in_Your_Ear_Searching_for_the_Causes_of_Poor_Singing).
8. **Handle octave errors kindly.** If the detected pitch is about 1200 cents off the target (a correct pitch class in the wrong octave), say "right note, different octave" rather than scoring it as a large miss. This matters most for men hearing higher guides. Evidence: [anecdotal choir practice (Rowbury)](https://blog.chrisrowbury.com/2016/05/how-male-singers-can-successfully-pitch.html). This is a design inference; no adult study was retrieved.
9. **Vary the pitches practised**, and do not always use the same starting note in the same key. Evidence: [Welch 1985, variable practice beat fixed practice without feedback](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2014.00598/full); [Pfordresher & Greenspon 2025, rationale for wider-range training (results not retrieved)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10298649241289542).
10. **Emphasise short multi-note echo patterns** (3 to 5 notes), not just single held notes. Evidence: [visual feedback helped 4-note melodies but not single pitches, Berglin et al. 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/03057356211026730).
11. **Report consistency (precision) as well as average miss and flat/sharp tendency.** For example, "your spread across repeats: ±X cents." Include this in "Copy results for Claude." Evidence: [Pfordresher et al. 2010, imprecision more common than inaccuracy](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/jasa/article/128/4/2182/683506/Imprecise-singing-is-widespread).
12. **Add a "guess, then reveal" step.** After a lane-off trial, ask "Were you flat, sharp or on?" before showing the result, and track how well the user self-assesses. This builds the internal error detection that must replace the app. Evidence: [self-evaluation plus model plus playback beats playback alone](https://www.ingentaconnect.com/contentone/fmea/rpme/2022/00000022/00000001/art00003); [Maas et al. 2008, KP matters until the learner can tell correct from incorrect](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1058-0360(2008/025)).
13. **Optional instant listen-back**, in memory only: model, then your take, then the model again. Evidence: [self-listening combined with a model and self-evaluation](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249808922_The_Effects_of_Modeling_Self-Evaluation_and_Self-Listening_on_Junior_High_Instrumentalist'_Music_Performance_and_Practice_Attitude); [audio playback improves self-assessment](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-95-1525-7_21).
14. **Songs: finish each song with a from-memory sing-through** with no guide audio and no lane (hide targets), scored afterwards. Singing from memory is a partly separate skill. Evidence: [Dalla Bella: memory as a source of poor singing; Sung Performance Battery phenotypes](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00714/full).
15. **Use external-focus cue wording** in lesson text ("send the sound to the far wall", "land the note on the line") and avoid anatomical instructions. Evidence: [Atkins & Duke 2013](https://acda-publications.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/IJRCS/volumefour/ijrcs4_2_atkinsduke.pdf); [Atkins 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0022429416673842). This evidence concerns tone rather than pitch.
16. **Headphone guidance:** keep guide volume low enough to hear yourself, or suggest one earbud out. Evidence: [masking reduces accuracy, Bottalico et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5010534/); [mild occlusion can improve self-monitoring](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0892199722003782).
17. **Onboarding message:** "Almost everyone can learn to sing in tune. True tone deafness is rare (about 1.5%), and pitch is a trainable ear-to-voice skill." This reduces the drop-off of self-labelled "tone-deaf" beginners. Evidence: [Peretz & Vuvan 2017 prevalence (cited in Pfordresher & Demorest 2021)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343166548_The_Prevalence_and_Correlates_of_Accurate_Singing); [Pfordresher & Brown 2007](http://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf); [Berglin et al. 2022, short training helps](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/03057356211026730).
18. **Let the user choose when to see feedback** ("show my line" button on advanced trials). This rests on general motor-learning research on self-controlled feedback; no singing-specific source was retrieved. Evidence: [motor-learning principles, Maas et al. 2008](https://gwulf.faculty.unlv.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/05/Maas-et-al_AJSLP-2008_PML-tutorial.pdf).

### Inferences
- The single highest-value change is probably items 1 and 2 together: lane-off trials plus faded feedback. The app would then measure and train the skill that transfers, instead of performance with the display on.
- Items 5 to 8 (echo-first, voice-like same-register guide, self-model, octave tolerance) are cheap in a plain JS/Web Audio app, and each rests on replicated pitch-matching findings.
- The app's current headphone behaviour (guide plays *while* singing) is the part most at odds with the evidence for poor-pitch adults. It should become a toggle, defaulting to echo or to guide-fades-out.

### Gaps
- No study has tested the full recommended schedule (concurrent → faded → terminal with bandwidth) for singing pitch. It is a synthesis from general and speech motor learning plus small singing studies.
- No retrieved study examined phone-app, self-guided adult learners with delayed retention and transfer tests.
- The optimal per-note tolerance band for beginners and its progression are not established empirically.
