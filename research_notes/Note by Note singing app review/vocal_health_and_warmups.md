# Vocal health, warm-ups and safe vocal load for adult beginner singers

Evidence tags used throughout: **[E]** = peer-reviewed empirical study or systematic review; **[P]** = professional/clinical guideline, established pedagogy or expert-educator consensus; **[M]** = marketing, clinic blog, popular article or anecdote.
Method note: the network proxy blocked full-text access to PubMed, PMC, ScienceDirect, jvoice.org, nats.org and voicescience.org, so the findings below come from abstracts and search-indexed summaries of those pages. Sample sizes and effect sizes are given only where the abstract stated them; where they were missing, this is listed under Gaps. Nothing has been filled in from memory.

## 1. Do vocal warm-ups (and cool-downs) help? Which kinds, and how long?

### Takeaway
Warm-ups have modest, fairly consistent short-term benefits: perceived singing ease improves, and some acoustic measures and choral intonation get better. The best-supported duration is **about 5 to 10 minutes**, and one study found 15 minutes was no better than none. Evidence that warm-ups prevent injury is absent. Cool-down evidence is thin and mostly subjective, though singers like them.

### Cited Findings
- [E] **Duration study (Journal of Voice, 2020/21):** first- and second-year collegiate classical voice majors did 0, 5, 10 or 15 minutes of warm-up, one week apart, then sang *Caro mio ben*. Self-rated singing difficulty on the EASE scale dropped significantly after **5 or 10 minutes** compared with **no warm-up or 15 minutes**. Female singers' frequency range also increased after the 5- and 10-minute warm-ups. — [PubMed 32891479](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32891479/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/344194995_Quantifying_Subjective_and_Objective_Measures_of_Singing_After_Different_Warm-Up_Durations)
- [E] **Survey of practice (Gish, Kunduk, Sims & McWhorter, J Voice 2012):** 117 voice students and professional singers took part. The most preferred warm-up length was 5 to 10 minutes, and most spent 5 to 20 minutes. Only 54% always warmed up, and 22% used a cool-down. The most common exercises were ascending and descending 5-note scales, octave scales, legato arpeggios and glissandi. — [PubMed 21439780](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21439780/)
- [E] **Amir et al. (J Voice 2005, 19:252–260):** after warm-up, frequency and amplitude perturbation (jitter and shimmer) fell, singer's-formant amplitude rose, and noise-to-harmonic ratio improved. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7835038_Evaluating_the_Influence_of_Warmup_on_Singing_Voice_Quality_Using_Acoustic_Measures)
- [E] **Motel et al. (2003):** 10 sopranos aged 19 to 21 compared a short, submaximal warm-up with vocal rest. Warm-up *increased* phonation threshold pressure (PTP, the minimum lung pressure needed to start the folds vibrating) at **high pitch** (p = 0.033), with no significant change at comfortable or low pitch. This runs against the usual belief that warm-up makes phonation easier. — [PubMed 12825648](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12825648/)
- [E] **McHenry et al. (J Voice 2009):** 20 actors (10 men, 10 women) did a vocal-only warm-up and a vocal-plus-aerobic warm-up in counterbalanced order. Men showed no difference in PTP change between the two. Women had a larger PTP reduction with the combined warm-up. — [J Voice abstract](https://www.jvoice.org/article/S0892-1997(08)00004-0/abstract)
- [E] **Choral warm-ups (J Voice 2017/18):** three choirs compared three warm-up procedures. After a combined physical and vocal warm-up, long-term average spectra showed more resonant singing, and all three choirs sang most in tune (least pitch deviation). — [PubMed 28483445](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28483445/)
- [E] **Warm-up vs just singing (J Voice, 2026):** a study compared structured warm-up with continuous singing. Women's jitter fell more after the warm-up, while men had lower values after continuous singing. The sex differences and the small number of measures limit how far this generalises. — [PubMed 41927414](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41927414/)
- [E] **Scoping review (J Voice 2025):** 20 studies of SOVT exercises, vocal function exercises and structured warm-ups in **trained** singers. The authors conclude that evidence-based vocal exercises enhance vocal health and performance. — [PubMed 41058356](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41058356/)
- [E] Studies of popular and CCM singers report improved EASE scores and harmonic-to-noise ratio after warm-up. A 2018 study compared a "physiological" warm-up (SOVT-based) with a "traditional" one in CCM singers. — [ScienceDirect 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199723000747); [ResearchGate, physiological vs traditional](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/317280904_Comparison_of_Effects_Produced_by_Physiological_Versus_Traditional_Vocal_Warm-up_in_Contemporary_Commercial_Music_Singers)
- [E] **Cool-down, Gottliebson (2011 PhD dissertation, University of Cincinnati):** after rigorous voice use, elite singers used a structured cool-down, vocal rest or conversation. Singers reported better vocal function after the cool-down. Outcomes measured were PTP, acoustic accuracy of tone and subjective perception. — [ADS abstract](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PhDT........84G/abstract)
- [E] **Cool-down, Ragan (J Voice 2016):** almost 80% of participating singers perceived a positive effect of cool-down exercises on their speaking and singing voice. Listener perception was also assessed. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199715002271); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/289494128_The_Impact_of_Vocal_Cool-down_Exercises_A_Subjective_Study_of_Singers'_and_Listeners'_Perceptions)
- [P] Ragan's 2018 *Journal of Singing* review ("The Efficacy of Vocal Cool-down Exercises") is the main pedagogy-facing summary of cool-down evidence. — [Free Online Library](https://www.thefreelibrary.com/The+Efficacy+of+Vocal+Cool-down+Exercises-a0539774139)

### Inferences
- The app's warm-up is about 3 minutes, which is shorter than the 5 to 10 minutes with the best (still limited) support. The evidence does not say 3 minutes is useless. It only shows that 5 and 10 minutes beat 0 and 15 in one sample of trained students.
- More warm-up is not always better. The 15-minute arm was no better than none, and Motel's high-pitch PTP rise suggests fatigue or other effects near the top of the range. A short, submaximal warm-up that stays mostly in mid-range is defensible.
- Nearly all warm-up studies used trained singers, actors or SLP students, and measured effects immediately. None tested adult beginners using an app, and none measured injury prevention. The app should present warm-ups as helping singing feel easier and more in tune, not as preventing injury.
- The choral study suggests warm-ups improve intonation, which is exactly what the app scores. So a warm-up plausibly improves the user's first-lesson scores, and the app could say so honestly.
- Cool-downs are cheap and unlikely to harm, but the benefit is mostly self-reported. They are reasonable to offer as optional, not as a requirement.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm the sample size of the 2020 duration study, the Amir 2005 study or the 2026 warm-up vs continuous-singing study, because full text was blocked.
- I found no study on warm-ups in **untrained adult beginners**, and none on warm-up and injury incidence.
- I found no controlled trial of cool-downs with objective outcomes beyond Gottliebson's dissertation.
- It is unclear whether the "physiological vs traditional" CCM study (2018) favoured either approach. I could only see its title.

## 2. Semi-occluded vocal tract (SOVT) exercises: mechanism, evidence, protocols, and whether an app can guide or verify them

### Takeaway
Titze's acoustic theory is well established. Partly closing the mouth (straw, lip trill, hum, "ng") adds "inertive reactance" and back-pressure, which lowers the pressure needed to start phonation and favours efficient, low-impact vibration. In healthy adults, a 4-week daily straw protocol reduced PTP, and straw length made no difference. In voice disorders, SOVT is not clearly better than other therapies. For healthy beginners, SOVT is a low-risk, well-reasoned warm-up choice rather than a proven performance enhancer.

### Cited Findings
- [E/P] **Mechanism (Titze):** the narrowed outlet reflects part of the sound wave back down the vocal tract. The inertive component of vocal-tract reactance feeds energy back into vibration and lowers oscillation threshold pressure. Titze's computer modelling asked whether "vocal economy" (high output with low intraglottal impact stress) can be raised with an artificially lengthened vocal tract. — [ResearchGate, Titze 2006 modelling](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5927019_Can_vocal_economy_in_phonation_be_increased_with_an_artificially_lengthened_vocal_tract_A_computer_modeling_study); [PMC, PTP with a semi-occluded tract](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4629990/)
- [E] **Healthy adults, sustained protocol (J Voice 2025):** 22 people with normal voices did **4 weeks of daily straw phonation**, randomly assigned a straw length of 7.5, 15 or 30 cm. PTP fell significantly, meaning phonation became easier. The effect of daily practice lasted **more than 1 day but less than 7 days** after stopping. **Straw length made no difference.** — [PMC12353171](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12353171/); [PubMed 40287308](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40287308/)
- [E] **Dysphonia meta-analysis (J Voice 2021):** compared with controls, SOVT improved fundamental frequency and subglottic pressure, but had significantly *negative* effects on shimmer and self-assessment. SOVT-based therapy was "not to be considered significantly superior" to other treatments. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199721001958); [ASHA Evidence Map summary](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/5baa3e22-e96e-4b14-a922-e848d530023f)
- [E] A 2025 systematic review of free-end straw training in dysphonic adults measured jitter, shimmer, HNR, CPP, F0 and contact quotient. I could only see its outcome list, not its conclusions. — [PubMed 39939185](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39939185/)
- [E] **Tubes in water (water resistance therapy):** the Finnish resonance-tube tradition dates from Sovijärvi in the 1960s, using glass tubes 24 to 28 cm long with an 8 to 9 mm inner diameter. LaxVox is a silicone-tube variant created by Marketta Sihvo in the early 1990s. A systematic review in singers found that phonating into glass tubes in water, straws and LaxVox tubes gave more comfortable phonation, better projection and more economical voice production. — [ResearchGate SR](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/324933143_The_Effects_of_Phonation_Into_Glass_Plastic_and_LaxVox_Tubes_in_Singers_A_Systematic_Review); [Dialnet review](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/7019830.pdf)
- [E] Guzmán and colleagues studied water resistance therapy **as a warm-up** for CCM singers, and ran a randomised controlled trial of it for behavioural dysphonia. — [PubMed 31060037](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31060037/); [PubMed 27769697](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27769697/)
- [E] Tube diameter and water depth change back-pressure and bubble characteristics. Deeper water and narrower tubes mean more resistance. — [ScienceDirect 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199717300401)
- [E] A 2024 glottal-imaging study compared ordinary vowels with SOVT exercises **produced loudly**. Its title implies that the benefit of SOVT depends on how it is done, but I could not read the results. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0892199724002005)
- [M] Teacher and commercial explanations repeat the same mechanism: the narrowed opening "sends part of the sound wave back down the tract." — [Ted's Voice Academy](https://tedsvoiceacademy.com/blog/what-is-straw-phonation-sovt-guide/)

### Inferences
- The app's sirens are voiced on "ng" or a lip trill. These are SOVT postures, so the app already follows the SOVT rationale, and the evidence supports keeping SOVT glides first in the warm-up.
- Straw length does not matter in healthy users, so the app can simply say "any ordinary drinking straw" instead of prescribing sizes.
- The benefit fades within a week, so regular brief practice fits the evidence better than occasional long sessions.
- **Can the app verify SOVT with microphone pitch tracking?** No study addresses this. On acoustic principles:
  - **Hum and "ng":** these give a strong periodic signal that normal pitch detectors track well.
  - **Lip trills:** these add amplitude and frequency modulation at the lip-flutter rate, which may cause dropouts.
  - **Straw phonation:** the output is much quieter.
  - **Straw in water:** this adds broadband bubble noise.
  - **What pitch cannot tell you:** pitch tracking can check that the user glided smoothly across the intended pitch span, but it cannot confirm the semi-occlusion itself. A straw and an open "oo" sound alike to the pitch tracker.
  - **Scoring:** SOVT steps should be scored leniently, or only on completion, so users are not penalised for tracking gaps.

### Gaps
- There are no studies of app-guided or self-guided SOVT, and no studies of SOVT specifically in untrained beginners (the 2025 healthy-adult study is the closest).
- Pitch-detector performance on lip trills and straw-in-water phonation needs testing on real iPhone input. I found no literature on it.
- I could not read the results of the loud-SOVT imaging study. It may bear on whether users should be told to keep SOVT exercises soft (softness is the common pedagogical advice, but I have no source).

## 3. Safe practice duration and vocal dose; signs of fatigue or misuse; when to see an ENT or speech pathologist

### Takeaway
No evidence-based "safe minutes per day" exists for untrained singers. What exists:
- Loading studies show fatigue builds with dose, and voices recover about 90% within 4 to 6 hours and fully within 12 to 18 hours.
- Spikes in load predict worse-feeling voices for 1 to 3 days.
- The clearest clinical threshold is the 2018 AAO-HNS guideline: hoarseness that does not resolve or improve within **4 weeks** should be examined (laryngoscopy), or sooner if a serious cause is suspected. This replaced the 2009 guideline's allowance of up to 3 months.

### Cited Findings
- [E] **Recovery (Hunter & Titze 2009, Annals of Otology, Rhinology & Laryngology):** 86 adults did a 2-hour vocal loading exercise (oral reading) and tracked recovery for 2 days. They reached about **90% recovery within 4 to 6 hours** and **full recovery at 12 to 18 hours**. The recovery trajectory resembled chronic-wound healing. — [PMC3311979](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3311979/); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/000348940911800608)
- [E] **Dosimetry in singers (Carroll et al. 2006, Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery):** 7 classical singers were monitored with the NCVS dosimeter over 2 weeks. Spikes in vocal load were followed by harsher self-ratings the same day **and 24 to 72 hours later**. When at least **48 hours of vocal rest** came before a load, ratings after the load were better. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0194599806027835)
- [E] Researchers use dose-based vocal loading tasks to induce fatigue experimentally, and use the Vocal Fatigue Index (VFI) as a validated self-report questionnaire. — [PMC7055723, dose-based loading](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7055723/); [ResearchGate, VFI development](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/273833040_Vocal_Fatigue_Index_VFI_Development_and_Validation)
- [E] One study compared collision and phonation threshold pressures before and after loud, prolonged vocalisation in trained and untrained voices, which implies different fatigue responses by training status. I could see only the title. — [ScienceDirect 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199713000544)
- [P] **AAO-HNS Clinical Practice Guideline: Hoarseness (Dysphonia) update (Stachler et al., 2018):**
  - Clinicians should perform laryngoscopy, or refer to someone who can, when dysphonia **fails to resolve or improve within 4 weeks**, or at any time if a serious underlying cause is suspected.
  - The 2009 guideline allowed up to 3 months, so **the 2009 threshold is superseded**.
  - CT or MRI should not come before the larynx has been visualised.
  - [AAFP summary](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2018/1115/p606.html); [AAO-HNS guideline page](https://www.entnet.org/quality-practice/quality-products/clinical-practice-guidelines/hoarseness-dysphonia/); [SAGE full citation](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0194599817751030)
- [P] **Voice rest (Sataloff, Cline, Lyons, Skeffington & Rubin, *Journal of Singing* 2019, NATS):** absolute voice rest means total silence. In the past it was prescribed for weeks (6 or more) for anything from nodules to laryngitis. Current expert opinion is that such prolonged absolute rest is not appropriate and may cause muscle deconditioning. — [NATS JOS "Voice Rest" PDF](https://www.nats.org/_Library/JOS_On_Point/JOS-075-5-2019-557_-_Voice_Rest_-_Satalof_-_Cline_-_Lyons_-_Skeffington_-_Rubin.pdf); [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vocal_rest)
- [E] **Whispering (Rubin et al. 2006):** laryngoscopy during whispering showed **69%** of patients had *more* supraglottic hyperfunction when whispering, 18% no change and 13% less. This means "whispering is always worse" is an overstatement, but it is worse for most people. — [PubMed 16503476](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16503476/)
- [P] **Laryngitis (UK patient information):** performers who resume singing too early, while symptoms are only easing, may prolong their hoarseness. Soft, sighing speech is better than whispering until the laryngitis has gone. — [patient.info](https://patient.info/ears-nose-throat-mouth/laryngitis-leaflet)
- [P] **Throat clearing:** vocal-hygiene guidance advises against habitual forceful throat clearing and suggests sipping water or swallowing instead. — [Johns Hopkins "Vocal Hygiene for Singers"](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/-/media/entaa-care/documents/vocal-hygiene/vocal-hygiene-for-singers.pdf); [RCSLT Voice Care factsheet](https://www.rcslt.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/RCSLT-Voice-Care-factsheet.pdf)
- [P] **Australia:** speech pathologists treat voice disorders, and Speech Pathology Australia's "Find a Speech Pathologist" search can filter by clinical area, including voice. — [healthdirect](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/amp/article/speech-pathology); [Better Health Channel](https://www.betterhealth.vic.gov.au/health/servicesandsupport/speech-pathologists)
- [M] Australian and US voice clinics describe singers as at higher risk of nodules, muscle tension dysphonia, reflux and polyps. They describe voice therapy for singers as combining resonant voice, breath work, SOVT and vocal hygiene. This is clinic marketing, but it matches mainstream practice. — [Connected Speech Pathology](https://connectedspeechpathology.com/blog/voice-therapy-for-singers); [VocaLab Voice Clinic (Melbourne)](https://vocalab.com.au/vocalab-voice-clinic/)

### Inferences
- For a beginner using the app, the practical dose rule from this evidence is:
  - Short, frequent sessions.
  - Stop when the voice feels tired.
  - Expect overnight recovery. If the voice has not recovered by the next morning, rest and do not practise.
- Hunter & Titze's 12 to 18 hour figure supports the "next morning" check. Carroll's 24 to 72 hour lag supports a lighter day after a big one.
- A "15 to 30 minutes" cap for beginners is a pedagogical convention, not a finding. If the app suggests a cap, it should say so. (I found no citable source for a specific minute figure; see Gaps.)
- The warning signs the app should name are:
  - Pain or soreness when singing or after.
  - Hoarseness or a husky voice that lasts.
  - Lost top notes, or a range that does not come back overnight.
  - More effort needed than usual.
  - Frequent throat clearing.
  - Voice breaks or airiness that is new.

  The self-report literature (fatigue, discomfort, symptoms improving with rest) supports these, and the 2018 guideline gives the escalation threshold: 4 weeks, or sooner if serious signs appear.
- The Australian pathway for a user is: see a GP, who can refer to an ENT or laryngologist, or see a speech pathologist directly. Telling users about the SPA "Find a Speech Pathologist" tool is concrete and local.
- The weekly streak could push users to sing while hoarse or sick. The laryngitis guidance and Carroll's load-spike data argue for explicit "rest days" that do not break the streak.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed safe daily singing duration for untrained adults, and no dose-response study in beginners.
- I could not read the AAO-HNS list of "serious underlying cause" red flags (for example smoking history, neck mass, recent intubation or surgery, stridor) from primary text. Confirm it before quoting it in the app.
- I could not access the VFI items or cut-off scores. Check the licensing and item wording before adapting them.
- There is no evidence on the sudden-loss-of-voice-after-loud-use red flag (possible vocal-fold haemorrhage) from sources I could open. It is a common laryngology teaching point, but it is unsourced here.

## 4. Range-extension safety: range tests, sirens and glides, loudness

### Takeaway
Voice-science guidance for range testing is to start low and soft, not to push, and to stop with any pain or strain. Warm-up extends measured range, at least in women, and range measured cold differs from range measured warm. I found no empirical literature on "safe" rates of range extension. Gradual extension is pedagogical tradition. Loud or extreme singing at the edges of the range is where studies show threshold-pressure and fatigue effects.

### Cited Findings
- [P] **Voice range profile (VRP, or phonetogram):** measures pitch range and soft-to-loud dynamic range at each pitch. There are three ways to do it:
  - A siren glide from the lowest comfortable note to the highest (quick).
  - A full protocol at every semitone, soft and loud.
  - A short protocol at every major third.

  Guidance: "start low and not very warmed up", because the lowest notes may be unavailable after extensive high singing. Do the softest singing first at each pitch. "If you feel any pain, strain, or discomfort during testing, stop immediately and rest your voice." — [VoiceScience.org, "How to Find Your Vocal Range"](https://www.voicescience.org/articles/how-to-test-your-vocal-range/); [VoiceScience.org VRP tool](https://www.voicescience.org/tools/vrp/)
- [E] **Short VRP protocol pilot (J Voice 2021):** a shortened protocol sampling every major third gave dynamic-range information without a full session. — [J Voice abstract](https://www.jvoice.org/article/S0892-1997(21)00146-6/abstract)
- [P] NATS publishes a "Voice Range Profile" resource in its Science-Informed Voice Pedagogy collection. I could not open it. — [NATS resource](https://www.nats.org/_Library/Science_Informed_Voice_Pedagogy_Resource/Voice_Range_Profile_docx.pdf)
- [E] **Warm-up changes measured range:** women's frequency range increased after 5 and 10 minutes of warm-up. — [PubMed 32891479](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32891479/)
- [E] **High pitch is where warm-up raised PTP:** in Motel 2003, warm-up raised threshold pressure only at high pitch. — [PubMed 12825648](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12825648/)
- [E] Glissandi (glides) are among the four most common warm-up exercises singers report. — [Gish et al. 2012, PubMed 21439780](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21439780/)
- [M] Consumer "vocal range test" sites encourage users to find their "real voice type in minutes" from lowest and highest notes. This conflates extreme range with usable range and voice type. — [vocalrangetest.org](https://vocalrangetest.org/); [vocalrangetest.com](https://vocalrangetest.com/)

### Inferences
- The app already asks for a **comfortable** low and high note ("Low, but not growly", "Not a squeak"). This matches the VRP safety advice and is better than consumer sites that ask for extremes. Keep that framing.
- It follows that:
  - Take the low note first and before any warm-up (the app already does), and take the high note after a brief warm-up for a more representative result.
  - Allow re-testing, because range changes with warm-up, time of day and practice.
  - Keep lesson material inside the comfortable range, not at its edges.
- The app's `sirenStep` widens across three rounds until it reaches within about 1 semitone of both measured extremes. That code has sirens as the very first warm-up step, so the user touches the top of their range in the first minute. The evidence (Motel's high-pitch PTP effect and the "start low, soft first" guidance) and tradition both favour starting glides in the middle of the range and widening later. This is a small, specific change.
- Loudness: the app cannot measure loudness in dB SPL with an uncalibrated iPhone mic, but it can detect *relative* level. It can at least tell users to sing at a comfortable, medium-soft level and avoid shouting for high notes. I found no source giving numeric loudness limits for beginners.

### Gaps
- I found no empirical study on how fast range can safely be extended, or on range-extension injury risk in beginners. Guidance here rests on tradition, and I found no citable text in the time available (for example Miller, McKinney or Chapman).
- I found no evidence on self-administered range tests causing harm.
- I found no citable numeric loudness guidance for beginners.

## 5. Special populations: adolescents, older adults, reflux, asthma, post-illness, pregnancy

### Takeaway
- **Adolescents:** the voice goes through staged changes (Cooksey for boys, Gackle for girls). Teens should not force range or be held to a fixed range.
- **Older adults:** there is growing evidence that singing-based and voice-exercise programmes *improve* aging voices, so older users are a positive case.
- **Asthma:** inhaled corticosteroids commonly cause reversible dysphonia.
- **Pregnancy:** it can reduce upper range and increase reflux.
- **After illness:** returning too early can prolong laryngitis.

For most of these groups, the right app response is a short disclaimer plus "listen to your body" adaptations, not exclusion.

### Cited Findings
- [P] **Adolescent boys (Cooksey):** the framework gives 5 stages of male voice change with stage-specific ranges and tessituras. Stage 3, "mutational climax", is when cracking into falsetto is most likely. Teachers should re-check voices regularly and choose repertoire to suit the current stage. — [VoiceScience.org, Male Voice Change](https://www.voicescience.org/lexicon/male-voice-change/); [Vocal Process](https://vocalprocess.co.uk/adolescent-voice-teenage-male-singers/); [Cooksey stages table](https://www.academia.edu/figures/29664770/table-1-cooksey-stages-of-male-voice-change)
- [P/E] **Adolescent girls (Gackle):** Gackle is the main authority on female voice change and describes 4 stages involving register breaks, timbre changes and "weight" changes. Sweet (2018, *Journal of Research in Music Education*) studied girls' experiences of voice change. — [Sweet 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429418763790); [ResearchGate figure based on Gackle 2000](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/a-Stages-of-singing-voice-change-for-females-based-on-Gackle-2000-and-males-based-on_fig2_279063526)
- [E] **Older adults, systematic review (JSLHR 2024):** singing-based interventions to improve physical functions of the aging voice. Five studies showed statistically significant improvements, three with large effect sizes. — [JSLHR 2024](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2024_JSLHR-23-00641)
- [E] **Korean older adults (J Voice 2020):** a therapeutic singing group improved significantly on peak expiratory flow, maximum phonation time, intensity, F0, jitter and shimmer. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199720302332)
- [P/E] **Presbyphonia:** it is the most common cause of dysphonia in people over 65, and SLP voice therapy is first-line. Voice therapy for presbyphonia improves patient-reported, aerodynamic, acoustic and expert-rated outcomes. — [ASHA Perspectives 2022, "Reframing Aging Voice and Presbyphonia"](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2022_PERSP-22-00023); [Systematic review of SLP interventions for presbyphonia (J Voice 2023)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S089219972300396X)
- [E] In a single-subject study of 6 older adults with presbyphonia (2025), all improved on primary outcomes after PhoRTE (Phonation Resistance Training Exercises) combined with inspiratory muscle strength training. — [PubMed 41176465](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41176465/)
- [E] **Asthma and inhaled corticosteroids (ICS):**
  - Dysphonia has been reported in up to **55%** of ICS users.
  - Mechanisms include candida laryngitis and vocalis-muscle atrophy.
  - Higher dose and more frequent use make it worse.
  - The dysphonia is usually reversible.
  - Standard-particle-size ICS was associated with more vocal fold atrophy than small-particle ICS.
  - A 2025 J Voice systematic review addresses ICS and voice quality; I saw the title only.
  - [ScienceDirect, ICS update 2005](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199705001116); [ScienceDirect, particle size](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199719303984); [ScienceDirect, SR 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199725001572); [PMC, laryngeal dysfunction in asthmatic women on ICS](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9734276/)
- [E, case level] **Pregnancy:** a J Voice case study reports pregnancy effects on the singing voice. — [ScienceDirect 2010](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199710001803)
- [M] Secondary sources say that in late pregnancy, raised sex-steroid hormones can cause fluid in Reinke's space (vocal fold oedema). This lowers F0, reduces high notes and changes timbre. Relaxation of the lower oesophageal sphincter also increases reflux, with throat clearing and voice change. These sources advise not trying to extend range during pregnancy. — [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/health/Voice-Changes-and-Pregnancy.aspx); [Center for Vocal Health (ENT clinic)](https://www.centerforvocalhealth.com/tips-from-the-doc/pregnancy-amp-the-voice-how-pregnancy-affects-vocal-health)
- [P] **After a cold or laryngitis:** resuming singing too early may prolong hoarseness. — [patient.info](https://patient.info/ears-nose-throat-mouth/laryngitis-leaflet); [Mayo Clinic, laryngitis](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/laryngitis/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20374267)
- [M] Choir-blog guidance recommends a graded return after colds or flu. — [Festival Singers blog, 2025](https://www.festivalsingers.org/vocal-health-blog/2025/11/4/singers-recovering-from-cold-or-flu)
- [E] A PMC review "Singing in and out of COVID" covers singing after COVID-19. I could not read it. — [PMC9877761](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9877761/)

### Inferences
- **Teens:** the app targets adults. If teens use it, the range test and transposition already adapt to their current range, which is good. But a teen in mid-change needs re-testing often and should not be pushed to hold a previous range. A short "if you're under 18 and your voice is changing, redo the range test often and never force notes" note covers this.
- **Older adults:** this is an encouraging group. Evidence suggests regular singing can improve voice function. The app should avoid alarmist messaging for them, and should still point to a GP or SLP for persistent change.
- **Asthma:** the app should not give medical advice. A neutral line is appropriate: "If you use a preventer inhaler and notice hoarseness, mention it to your GP or pharmacist." The link between ICS and dysphonia is well documented.
- **Pregnancy:** expect temporary range loss, especially at the top. Don't chase high notes. Re-test the range. Evidence is weak (case studies and secondary sources).
- **Reflux:** I found no strong singer-specific evidence in my searches. Keep any mention minimal: frequent throat clearing, a morning husky voice or a burning throat are worth raising with a GP.

### Gaps
- I found no primary study quantifying reflux (LPR) prevalence or effects in singers, or specific to beginners.
- I have no primary text for Cooksey's or Gackle's original works. Stage details come from secondary teaching sources.
- I found no evidence on singing guidance after COVID-19 or long COVID, beyond the unread PMC title.
- Pregnancy evidence is case-level plus secondary summaries. I found no controlled studies.

## 6. Hydration, steam, humidity and other vocal-hygiene claims: evidence vs myth

### Takeaway
Hydration's role in vocal fold function has lab support, both systemic (drinking water) and surface (breathing humid air or nebulised saline). Nebulised isotonic saline and steam can reduce perceived dryness and effort, with small or inconsistent objective effects in healthy young adults. **Caffeine avoidance is not supported** by a systematic review, though many handouts still recommend it. Most hygiene advice is reasonable but rests on thin evidence.

### Cited Findings
- [E] **Hydration review (Sivasankar & Leydon, 2010):** there is some evidence that systemic and superficial hydration promote laryngeal health, but more research is needed to validate clinical recommendations. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/43131568_The_role_of_hydration_in_vocal_fold_physiology)
- [E] **Vocal hygiene programmes for singers, systematic review (J Voice, 2023/24):** 4 studies were included, 2 on hydration as treatment and 2 on hygiene instruction. They showed improvements in maximum phonation time, intensity, Dysphonia Severity Index and number of daily vocal breaks taken. The evidence base is very small. — [PubMed 38052688](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38052688/)
- [E] **Nebulised saline in healthy men (Tanner et al., J Voice ~2016):** double-blind, within-subjects design with 10 university-trained male singers and 10 non-singers, mean age 21.8. After a laryngeal desiccation challenge, participants received 3 or 9 mL of nebulised isotonic saline. There were **no physiological voice changes** from desiccation or saline, but self-reported effort linked to dryness "might improve" with nebulisation. — [PubMed 26412295](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26412295/)
- [E] **Multidimensional nebulisation study (J Voice 2021):** after 22 minutes of breathing dry air, 10 minutes of nebulised hypotonic, isotonic (0.9%) or hypertonic (3%) saline increased vocal fold mucosal wave in women and men, and improved aerodynamic efficiency in men. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199721000837)
- [E] **Steam vs nebulisation:** in a randomised comparison, steam inhalation improved NHR, shimmer and jitter, while only nebulised isotonic saline lowered phonation threshold pressure. — [ResearchGate, steam inhalation study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/298211966_Effects_of_steam_inhalation_on_voice_quality-related_acoustic_measures)
- [E] **Caffeine, systematic review (J Voice 2021):** little evidence supports the belief that caffeine harms the voice, and clinicians "should be cautious when recommending caffeine abstinence." — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0892199721000849)
- [M] Popular singing sites still say caffeine harms the voice or dehydrates the folds, contradicting the systematic review. — [LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/is-caffeine-bad-for-singers-2994147)
- [M] Voice-trainer blogs explain the topical-vs-systemic hydration distinction in lay terms. — [Vocal Process](https://vocalprocess.co.uk/hydration-vocal-folds/); [BAST Training](https://basttraining.com/blog/the-truth-about-singing-and-hydration/)
- [M] Commercial "vocal steamers", "vocal mist" products and throat pastilles are sold with performance claims that go beyond the evidence above. — [Forbrain steamer guide](https://www.forbrain.com/how-to-sing-better/best-singing-tools/vocal-steamers/); [Provoice VocalMist](https://provoicecare.net/goodbye-vocal-dryness-mucus-hello-vocalmist/); [Vocalzone (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vocalzone_Throat_Pastilles)
- [P] Johns Hopkins, RCSLT and similar handouts recommend these basics: drink water, limit throat clearing, avoid shouting, and rest when hoarse. — [Johns Hopkins](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/-/media/entaa-care/documents/vocal-hygiene/vocal-hygiene-for-singers.pdf); [RCSLT](https://www.rcslt.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/RCSLT-Voice-Care-factsheet.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Safe for the app to say:** drink water normally through the day; dry air, such as air-conditioning or heating, can make singing feel harder; breathing steam or humid air may make the voice feel easier.
- **The app should not:**
  - Tell users to avoid coffee or tea.
  - Promote sprays, lozenges or steamers as performance enhancers.
  - Imply hydration will fix hoarseness that lasts.
- Myths to avoid repeating as fact: that caffeine dehydrates the voice, and that whispering is always safer than speaking. The Rubin 2006 finding in section 3 is nuanced: whispering is harder for most people, but not all.

### Gaps
- I could not verify the sample size or year of the steam-vs-nebulisation study.
- I found no reliable evidence on dairy, alcohol, honey, ginger or menthol and the singing voice in the time available. Treat these as unverified tradition.
- I found no study of environmental humidity in Australian homes and singing specifically. The dry-air challenge studies are the closest analogue.

## Implications for a self-guided singing app

Each item gives the change, then the evidence it rests on, using the tags above.

1. **Offer a 5 to 10 minute warm-up and keep the 3-minute version as "Quick".** The current warm-up is "about three minutes". The best (limited) evidence favours 5 to 10 minutes, and 15 minutes was no better than none. *Evidence: [E] 2020 duration study, PubMed 32891479; [E] Gish 2012 survey, where 5 to 10 minutes was preferred.*

2. **Start the siren or glide step in the middle of the range and widen it gradually. Reach the top only in the last round or at the end of the warm-up.**
   - Today `sirenStep` spans 55% to 100% of the half-range minus 1 semitone, as the first warm-up step, so the user nears both extremes within the first minute.
   - Reordering helps: mid-range hums, then scales, then wider sirens, then long notes.
   - *Evidence: [E] Motel 2003 (high-pitch PTP rose after warm-up); [P] VoiceScience VRP guidance "start low, soft first"; range expanded after warm-up in the 2020 study.*

3. **Keep SOVT (hum, "ng", lip trill) as the opening, and add an optional straw-phonation step.**
   - Instruction: "any drinking straw", with a gentle, soft glide.
   - Straw-in-water can be an optional variant; shallower water gives less resistance.
   - Score SOVT steps on completion or smoothness only, because pitch tracking cannot confirm the occlusion and may drop out on lip trills or bubbles.
   - Test the detector on real iPhone lip-trill and straw input first.
   - *Evidence: [E] 2025 J Voice sustained straw study (PTP down, straw length irrelevant, benefit fades within 7 days); [E/P] Titze inertive-reactance mechanism; [E] resonance-tube systematic review in singers. Detection feasibility is an untested inference.*

4. **Add an optional 1 to 2 minute cool-down after lessons and songs.** Use descending glides, lip trills and gentle hums, and label it optional. *Evidence: [E] Gottliebson 2011 dissertation; [E] Ragan 2016, about 80% perceived benefit. Evidence is weak and self-reported, but the cost is low.*

5. **Replace or supplement the streak with rest-friendly mechanics.**
   - Add "rest day" and "unwell" days that do not break the weekly streak.
   - Never nag users to practise while hoarse or sick.
   - After a long session, suggest a lighter next day.
   - *Evidence: [E] Carroll 2006 (load spikes lead to worse ratings for 24 to 72 hours; 48 hours of rest before a load helped); [E] Hunter & Titze 2009 (full recovery takes 12 to 18 hours); [P] patient.info (singing too early after laryngitis prolongs hoarseness).*

6. **Track on-device daily singing minutes and nudge gently, but do not present a number as a safety limit.**
   - Example: after about 20 to 30 minutes of active singing, say "Good time to rest your voice."
   - State plainly that this is teacher convention, not a proven limit.
   - Keep everything on-device, as now.
   - *Evidence: I found no peer-reviewed beginner dose limit (see Gaps); [E] fatigue accumulates with dose (dosimetry, loading studies).*

7. **Add a one-tap "How does your voice feel?" check after sessions**, with the options Easy, OK, Tired and Sore/scratchy.
   - "Sore" shows rest guidance.
   - "Tired" twice in a row suggests a rest day.
   - Include this in "Copy results for Claude" so the AI coach factors in fatigue and does not just push accuracy.
   - *Evidence: [E] the EASE scale and Vocal Fatigue Index show self-report is the validated way to track singer fatigue; [E] Hunter & Titze (recovery is self-rated).*

8. **Add a plain-English "Look after your voice" screen, reachable from Settings or the range test, with red flags and an Australian referral path.**
   - Stop if singing hurts.
   - Rest if you're hoarse.
   - If hoarseness or a changed voice hasn't improved in **4 weeks**, see your GP. They can refer you to an ENT, or you can see a speech pathologist directly through Speech Pathology Australia's "Find a Speech Pathologist".
   - Get checked sooner if something feels seriously wrong.
   - *Evidence: [P] AAO-HNS 2018 guideline (4 weeks; replaces the 2009 3-month threshold); [P] healthdirect / Better Health Channel.* Before shipping, verify the guideline's serious-cause red-flag list from primary text.

9. **Range test refinements.**
   - Keep the "comfortable, not growly, not a squeak" wording.
   - Add "sing softly; if anything strains or hurts, stop."
   - Take the low note before any singing (already done) and the high note after the warm-up.
   - Show "Re-test your range" regularly, and prompt it after "Tired or Sore" days or when many notes land an octave off (the app already has a hint for this).
   - Never gamify "highest note ever".
   - *Evidence: [P] VoiceScience VRP guidance; [E] warm-up extends measured range (2020 study).*

10. **Keep lesson tessitura inside the comfortable range, not at its edges.** When transposing, keep the highest target note a semitone or two below the tested top for most lessons, and use full-range material only in the sirens lesson. *Evidence: [E] Motel 2003 high-pitch effect; [P] Cooksey-style tessitura thinking (fit material to current comfortable range). This is mainly pedagogical inference, and I found no direct trial.*

11. **Loudness cue.** Tell users to sing at a medium-soft, speaking-like level and never to push volume to reach high notes. The app could optionally warn if input level stays near clipping for long stretches, since relative level is detectable even without a calibrated mic. *Evidence: [P] vocal-hygiene guidance against shouting; [E] loud prolonged voicing is the standard fatigue-inducing task in loading studies. I found no beginner-specific numeric limit.*

12. **Hydration and hygiene copy should be evidence-true.**
    - Say: "Sip water through the day; dry air can make singing feel harder; breathing steam may make it feel easier."
    - Do **not** tell users to avoid coffee, tea or dairy.
    - Do not recommend sprays or lozenges.
    - Suggest a sip of water or a swallow instead of forceful throat clearing.
    - *Evidence: [E] caffeine systematic review 2021; [E] Tanner 2016 and 2021 nebulisation studies (perceived effort improves, objective effects are small); [E] Sivasankar & Leydon 2010; [P] Hopkins/RCSLT handouts.*

13. **Special-population notes, one line each, in onboarding or on the health screen:**
    - **Under 18 with a changing voice:** re-test your range often and never force notes.
    - **Pregnant:** your upper range may shrink for a while; don't chase high notes.
    - **Preventer inhaler users:** if you notice hoarseness, mention it to your GP or pharmacist.
    - **Older singers:** regular singing may help keep your voice strong. (Frame this positively.)
    - **After a cold or laryngitis:** wait until your voice feels normal, then start with the quick warm-up.
    - *Evidence: [P] Cooksey and Gackle; [M] and case-level pregnancy sources; [E] ICS dysphonia literature; [E] JSLHR 2024 singing and aging review; [P] patient.info.*

14. **Frame claims honestly and avoid over-promising.** Say warm-ups "help your voice feel easier and your pitch steadier", which is supported. Do not say they "prevent injury", which is unsupported. Make clear the app does not diagnose voice problems, and that its accuracy scores say nothing about vocal health. *Evidence: every warm-up study measured immediate acoustic or perceptual effects, and none measured injury (section 1 Gaps).*

15. **Guidance for the AI-coach export.** Add a short line to the copied text, such as: "Please don't diagnose; if I report pain or hoarseness lasting more than a few weeks, suggest a GP or speech pathologist." Include session minutes and the voice-feel check so the chatbot's advice considers vocal load, not just pitch accuracy. *Evidence: follows from items 5 to 8; there is no direct study of AI singing coaches.*
