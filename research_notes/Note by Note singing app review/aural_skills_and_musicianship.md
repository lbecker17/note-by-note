# Aural skills and musicianship training that support learning to sing (ear training, solfège, audiation, rhythm)

Scope note: this file covers how musicianship methods (Kodály, Gordon's Music Learning Theory, Curwen hand signs, movable-do solfège, numbers) build pitch memory, interval singing, tonal context and sight-singing, and what that means for "Note by Note". Research was done on 30 September 2026. Several publisher sites (SAGE, Springer, Frontiers, PubMed Central, the Buffalo APAL lab site) blocked full-text fetching, so some findings below come from abstracts and search snippets. Those are marked "(abstract/snippet only)".

Evidence tags used throughout:
- **[Empirical]**: a peer-reviewed or thesis study with data. Sample and effect are given where known.
- **[Survey]**: a practitioner survey (tells us what people do or believe, not what works).
- **[Pedagogy]**: an established teaching tradition or consensus from a named pedagogue or text. It is usually not experimentally tested.
- **[App/Marketing]**: the vendor's own description of its product or rationale.

Overall picture: the evidence base is small and skewed. Most controlled studies used **children** (Kodály, hand signs, accompaniment) or **school/college ensemble musicians** (solfège systems, drones). Studies of **adult beginners learning alone with an app** are close to non-existent. Most app-relevant implications therefore come from the psychology of adult vocal pitch imitation (Pfordresher, Dalla Bella and others), with pedagogy traditions filling the gaps.

## 1. What role does tonal context (drone, tonic, cadence, harmony underneath) play in pitch accuracy and learning? Drones; just vs equal temperament

### Takeaway
A key context clearly helps people **perceive and remember** pitches and intervals, and good sight-singers set up the key before they sing. However, controlled studies of **drones and harmonic accompaniment** mostly find **no measurable gain in produced intonation**, even though musicians strongly believe drones help. Unaccompanied singers tend to drift toward just intonation. An app should therefore set the key cheaply before every exercise (tonic or cadence, then the singer sings "do"), offer a drone as an optional aid rather than a promised fix, and score tolerantly around equal temperament.

### Cited Findings
**Tonal context and perception/memory**
- [Empirical] Graves & Oxenham (2017), *Frontiers in Psychology*, is titled "Familiar Tonal Context Improves Accuracy of Pitch Interval Perception". Its headline result is that intervals are judged more accurately inside a familiar (tonal) context than in isolation. Sample and effect size could not be retrieved (fetch blocked). — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01753/full)
- [Empirical] Adults and grade-school children encode and represent novel **atonal** melodies less accurately than **tonal** melodies (Saffran et al., 2003). — [Saffran 2003, Infant Learning Lab PDF](https://infantlearning.waisman.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/70/2017/02/Saffran2003a.pdf)
- [Empirical] Groulx (2013, *JRME*) found that pitch errors which broke the tonal framework were detected significantly more easily than errors in atonal contexts. — [Groulx 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022429413485602)
- [Empirical] Killian & Henry (2005, *JRME*; N = 198 high-school singers, individual sight-singing): high scorers **"tonicized"** (sang to establish the key) significantly more often than low scorers, both while practising and before performing. This is correlational: good readers do it, but the study did not assign tonicizing at random. — [Killian & Henry 2005](https://doi.org/10.1177/002242940505300105); summary at [Everyday Musicality](https://everydaymusicality.com/2019/05/29/successful-sight-singing-strategies-killian-henry-2005/)

**Drones (tonic pedal) and accompaniment underneath**
- [Empirical] Springer, Silvey, Nápoles & Warnet (2021, *JRME*): collegiate wind players played a melody with a mono drone (tonic), a dyad drone (tonic + fifth) and no drone. There was **no significant effect of drone condition on measured intonation**. Players' self-ratings did differ: 59.74% believed the dyad drone gave their best intonation, 28.57% the mono drone and 11.68% no drone. — [ERIC EJ1311395](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1311395); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022429420974389)
- [Empirical] Zabanal (2019): 28 middle and high school violin and viola players practised briefly with a tonic drone. There was no significant difference between the pretest, the drone-accompanied posttest and the unaccompanied posttest. — [Zabanal 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1948499219851407)
- [Survey] Most collegiate string players surveyed use drones, mostly for scales and arpeggios, and believe drones are effective. — [ResearchGate survey](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334447659_A_Survey_of_Collegiate_String_Musicians'_Use_of_Drone_Accompaniment_to_Improve_Intonation)
- [Empirical, children, older] Kuhn & Sims (1983) found no significant differences in singing accuracy between melodic, harmonic, combined, or no accompaniment. This is reported second-hand in the literature reviewed by Hinkley (2021) and Hedden (2012). — [Hinkley 2021, IJRCS](https://acda.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/IJRCSVol9Hinkley.pdf); [Hedden 2012](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/8755123312438516)
- [Empirical, children] Atterbury & Silcox (1993): kindergartners taught for a year with or without piano accompaniment showed no significant difference in singing ability. — [Atterbury & Silcox 1993](https://doi.org/10.2307/3345478)
- [Empirical, children] Guilbault (2004): K–1 children taught songs with a **root-melody accompaniment** (the chord roots under the tune) kept a better sense of pitch and tonality when **improvising**. The accompaniment did not significantly improve how accurately they sang the songs. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/258156020_The_Effect_of_Harmonic_Accompaniment_on_the_Tonal_Achievement_and_Tonal_Improvisations_of_Children_in_Kindergarten_and_First_Grade)

**What the guide sounds like (voice vs piano)**
- [Empirical] "Accuracy of pitch matching significantly improved by live voice model" (*Journal of Voice*, 2013): a live voice model improved pitch matching relative to comparison models. Sample and effect size were not retrieved (abstract/snippet only). — [PubMed 23528675](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23528675/)
- [Empirical] A literature review in a 2018 PMC paper on sung vs whistled imitation reports that singers match pitches and intervals much more accurately when repeating **sung** intervals than intervals played on **piano** (snippet only). — [PMC5936900](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5936900/)
- [Empirical] Nichols (2021, *Psychology of Music*) studied vocal vs piano **doubling** and children's accuracy. The result was not retrieved. — [Nichols 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735620936757)

**Just vs equal temperament in unaccompanied singing**
- [Empirical, small sample] Howard (2007, *Journal of Voice*) used specially composed chorales in which each chord holds over a note from the previous one. An experienced a cappella SATB quartet **drifted in pitch** as predicted if singers tune each new chord **justly** from the held notes. The study reports evidence that just intonation was preferred on average. The sample was one quartet. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199705001657); [ResearchGate: Equal or non-equal temperament in a cappella SATB singing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/6223729_Equal_or_non-equal_temperament_in_a_capella_SATB_singing)
- [Empirical] A later longitudinal study of an a cappella quintet (*Journal of Voice*, 2018) and a 2022 computational study of pitch drift in **solo** unaccompanied singing both exist. Their detailed results were not retrieved. — [J Voice longitudinal quintet](https://www.jvoice.org/article/S0892-1997(18)30241-8/fulltext); [arXiv 2204.01009](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.01009)
- [Survey/practitioner] A UK survey of pitch drift among choir directors (Seaton, Open University, via the Association of British Choral Directors, 2024) documents drift as a common practical problem. — [ABCD PDF](https://abcd.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Pitch-Drift-Seaton.pdf)

**App rationale**
- [App/Marketing] Functional Ear Trainer (Alain Benbassat method) plays an I–IV–V–I cadence before each test tone. The learner identifies the tone by its **function in the key** (scale degree) rather than as an isolated interval. The claimed benefit is that a tone's feel relative to the tonic transfers across keys. — [AdvancingMusician](https://advancingmusician.com/functional-ear-training/); [App Store AU](https://apps.apple.com/au/app/functional-ear-trainer/id1088761926)

### Inferences
- The asymmetry is consistent. Tonal context reliably helps **perception, memory and error detection**, but adding sound **underneath while producing** (drone, accompaniment) has repeatedly failed to show a measurable production benefit, at least in children and instrumentalists. The cheapest well-supported step is to establish the key **before** the exercise (tonic or cadence, and have the singer sing "do"). Continuous harmony during singing is less supported.
- Drones feel helpful and learners like them. Measured benefit is not shown, so a drone is fine as an **optional** practice mode. It should not be marketed as fixing intonation, and it should alternate with drone-off repetitions so the skill does not depend on it. Zabanal's unaccompanied posttest is the relevant check.
- Arithmetic on tuning: a just major third (5:4) is about 386 cents and an equal-tempered one is 400 cents, a 14-cent gap. A just major sixth (5:3) is about 884 cents vs 900. A singer tuning "sweetly" against a drone can therefore show a consistent ~14-cent flat third on an equal-tempered target. Scoring tolerance and "flat/sharp tendency" messages should not treat ~15 cents of flatness on mi or la as a fault. This matters most when a tonic drone is on.
- Across studies, a **voice-like** guide produces better matching than piano. This is relevant because Note by Note's guide tone is synthesized and piano-like.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found of **adult beginners** singing with vs without a drone or cadence primer and measuring pitch accuracy.
- The results of Morgan's dissertation on the effects of tonic drone **and singing** on string players' pitch accuracy were not retrieved. — [eGrove](https://egrove.olemiss.edu/etd/2448/)
- No reliable study was found on whether solo beginners gain from being scored against just rather than equal-tempered targets.
- Sample and effect sizes for Graves & Oxenham 2017 and the 2013 live-voice study could not be retrieved (fetch blocked).

## 2. Movable-do solfège vs numbers vs letter names vs lyrics/neutral syllables for beginners

### Takeaway
There is **no strong evidence that any one naming system produces better singers**. The best-controlled individual study found the system made no significant difference. Movable-do dominates practice in the US. What successful sight-singers share is **strategy** (establishing the key, keeping the beat, practising aloud) rather than a particular syllable set. Gordon's tradition starts with **neutral syllables** and adds names later. For an app, the choice rests on practical factors: singability, familiarity, and how well the system shows scale-degree function.

### Cited Findings
- [Empirical, correlational] Demorest & May (1995, *JRME*; N = 414 choir members at 4 Texas high schools): the two **movable-do** schools scored significantly higher than the two fixed-do schools. The authors cautioned that **teacher skill** was confounded with system. The strongest predictor of individual success was **years of school choir**, followed by years of piano, instrumental and vocal lessons. — [Demorest & May 1995](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.2307/3345676)
- [Empirical] Killian & Henry (2005; N = 198): the sight-singing system used made **no significant difference**. A 30-second practice opportunity raised accuracy significantly. — [Killian & Henry 2005](https://doi.org/10.1177/002242940505300105); [ACDA Michigan summary](https://www.acdami.org/successful-sight-singing-techniques/)
- [Empirical, unpublished dissertation; contested] A University of San Francisco doctoral study of 85 college music majors found that those trained in **fixed-do** had higher sight-singing pitch accuracy at all complexity levels, with very large effect sizes. Participants were grouped by prior training background rather than randomly assigned, so selection effects (e.g., conservatory backgrounds) are likely. This conflicts with Demorest & May. — [USF repository](https://repository.usfca.edu/diss/38/)
- [Survey] About 89% of teachers in one survey of high-achieving middle and high school choirs used movable-do for sight-singing (fixed-do 3.55%, letter names 0.71%). The figure appears in the 2020 *Research Perspectives in Music Education* article (snippet only). — [Ingenta](https://www.ingentaconnect.com/contentone/fmea/rpme/2020/00000021/00000001/art00003?crawler=true&mimetype=application%2Fpdf)
- [Pedagogy] Some teachers use scale-degree **numbers** (1 2 3 4 5 6 "sev" 1) instead of syllables. The source is a secondary study guide, which is a weak source. — [Fiveable](https://fiveable.me/fundamentals-of-music-theory/unit-11/sight-singing-techniques/study-guide/IaKtOukWLIpqbfzZ)
- [Pedagogy] In Gordon's Music Learning Theory "aural/oral" stage, tonal patterns are sung on a **neutral syllable** ("bum") and rhythm patterns on "bah", without names, before any verbal association or notation. — [The Improving Musician](https://theimprovingmusician.com/aural-oral-laying-the-foundation-for-musical-understanding/); [Wikipedia: Gordon MLT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_music_learning_theory)

**Curwen hand signs (often paired with movable-do)**
- [Empirical; mixed] Boisvert (*Research & Issues in Music Education*, vol. 15): kindergarten children aged 5–6 were taught with Curwen signs (n = 33) or without (n = 33). There was **no significant difference** in singing accuracy. — [JMU Commons](https://commons.lib.jmu.edu/rime/vol15/iss1/3/); [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1271551.pdf)
- [Empirical; mixed] Frey-Clark's (2017) literature review reports mixed results across studies. Some found gestures (including horizontal sweeping and opening–closing movements) helped children with intervals. In one study, solfège + hand signs and solfège alone both beat **letter names** on posttests. Others found no difference. — [Frey-Clark 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/8755123316689812)
- [Empirical] A second-grade thesis at Missouri State tested Curwen signs vs other hand movements. A 10-week first-grade study (n = 47) compared solfège + signs with solfège only. Neither gave a clear winner, per the snippet. — [Missouri State thesis](https://bearworks.missouristate.edu/theses/2665/)
- [Empirical] Killian & Henry (2005) found high scorers used hand signs more often (correlational). — [Killian & Henry 2005](https://doi.org/10.1177/002242940505300105)

### Inferences
- The evidence does not justify forcing solfège on adult beginners. It also does not justify avoiding it. The one repeated finding against **letter names** (in Frey-Clark's review) fits the theory: letter names are absolute, so after transposition to the singer's range they change every time. Movable-do or numbers keep the same label for the same function in every key.
- App-specific pros and cons (reasoning, not tested):
  - **Movable-do**: open vowels are easy to sing and sustain. It has a big teaching ecosystem (Sound of Music "Do-Re-Mi", Kodály resources) and hand signs. Minor-mode conventions (la-based vs do-based minor) confuse beginners.
  - **Numbers**: most intuitive for adults with no background, and they map directly to "scale degree". "Seven" has two syllables and many numbers have closed vowels, so they are harder to sustain.
  - **Neutral syllables ("loo", "nah")**: lowest cognitive load, and matching the pitch is the whole job. They build no vocabulary for function.
  - **Lyrics**: most motivating, but hardest for pitch accuracy because vowels and consonants change.
- Because Note by Note already **transposes every lesson** to the singer's key, labelling by function (movable-do or numbers) is the natural fit. Letter names would be misleading.

### Gaps
- No study was found comparing solfège vs numbers vs neutral syllables for **adult, self-taught** singers.
- The results of McClung's study, "Sight-Singing Scores of High School Choristers with Extensive Training in Movable Solfege Syllables and Curwen Hand Signs", were not retrieved. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249808856_Sight-Singing_Scores_of_High_School_Choristers_with_Extensive_Training_in_Movable_Solfege_Syllables_and_Curwen_Hand_Signs)
- No evidence was found on whether hand signs help when the learner is alone with a phone. No one is watching, and the phone is often held in one hand.

## 3. Audiation (Gordon): hearing internally before singing; how to train it; evidence that pre-hearing improves accuracy

### Takeaway
Audiation, meaning hearing and understanding music in the mind, is central to Gordon's theory and to Kodály's "inner hearing" practice. There is converging **correlational** evidence that inaccurate singers have weaker auditory imagery and that accurate singers prepare internally. The main modern account of poor-pitch singing is weak **sensorimotor mapping** (turning an internal pitch image into a vocal plan), not deafness. Direct experimental evidence that inserting a "hear it silently, then sing" step improves beginners' accuracy is **thin**. It is plausible, cheap to test, and consistent with the data.

### Cited Findings
- [Pedagogy] Gordon coined "audiation" in 1975. It means hearing and comprehending music when the sound is not physically present, and Gordon treats it as the foundation of musicianship. His learning sequence has learners imitate and audiate tonal and rhythm patterns and recognize a **tonal center** and macrobeats. — [Wikipedia: Gordon MLT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_music_learning_theory); [GIML: About MLT](https://giml.org/mlt/about/)
- [Empirical] Pfordresher & Halpern (2013, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*): participants imitated four-note monotone sequences. **Less accurate imitators reported less vivid auditory imagery** on the Bucknell Auditory Imagery Scale. Pitch discrimination did not explain the difference. The authors propose that vocal imitation relies on auditory imagery to plan laryngeal movements. — [PubMed 23413013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23413013/); [Author PDF](https://neuroscience.gsu.edu/files/2022/12/Auditory-imagery-and-the-poor-pitch-singer.pdf)
- [Empirical] Greenspon & Pfordresher (2019, *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics*) is titled "Pitch-specific contributions of auditory imagery and auditory memory in vocal pitch imitation". It separates imagery from short-term memory as contributors to imitation. Details were not retrieved (fetch blocked). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-019-01799-0)
- [Empirical] Greenspon, Pfordresher & Halpern (2017, *Music Perception*) studied pitch imitation when melodies must be **mentally transformed** before singing, which is an audiation-type task. Details were not retrieved. — [Music Perception](https://online.ucpress.edu/mp/article-abstract/34/5/585/62807/Pitch-Imitation-Ability-in-Mental-Transformations?redirectedFrom=fulltext)
- [Empirical] A 2025 *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics* paper on vocal-motor interference reports (snippet) that less accurate singers, who report less vivid imagery, make **more preparatory subvocal movements** than accurate singers. — [Springer 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-025-03073-y)
- [Empirical] Poor-pitch singing occurs **without** tone deafness. About 10–15% of participants in imitation experiments sing at least a semitone off, and these singers **compress interval sizes** (Pfordresher & Brown, 2007). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/237377955_Poor-Pitch_Singing_in_the_Absence_of_Tone_Deafness); [PMC5936900 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5936900/)
- [Empirical] "Singing ability is rooted in vocal-motor control of pitch" (Hutchins, Larrouy-Maestri & Peretz, 2014) supports a motor-control rather than perceptual account. This is from the title only; details were not retrieved. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-014-0732-1)
- [Empirical, not re-verified this session] Zarate et al. (2010, *PLoS ONE*) found that micromelody **discrimination** training improved discrimination but did not measurably improve **vocal** accuracy. This suggests listening-only ear training does not automatically transfer to singing. — [PMC2887372](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2887372/)
- [Empirical] Killian & Henry (2005): a 30-second preparation period raised sight-singing accuracy significantly. High scorers sang aloud during practice and finished preparing within 30 seconds. The period mixed silent and aloud rehearsal, so it is not a pure audiation test. — [Killian & Henry 2005](https://doi.org/10.1177/002242940505300105)
- [Pedagogy] The Gordon "aural/oral" stage is: teacher sings a pattern on a neutral syllable, learner audiates, then echoes. This is the classroom form of "hear, think, sing". — [The Improving Musician](https://theimprovingmusician.com/aural-oral-laying-the-foundation-for-musical-understanding/)

**Superseded or updated view**
- [Empirical] The old belief that many adults are "tone deaf" and cannot learn is superseded. In lab testing, about **85–90% of occasional singers sing in tune**, yet about 60% of more than 1,000 university students believed they could not imitate melodies accurately. — [Berkowska & Dalla Bella 2013, Frontiers (Sung Performance Battery)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00714/full); [Dalla Bella 2015 PDF](https://dallabella-lab.ca/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Dalla-Bella_2015_Defining-Poor-Pitch-Singing-A-Problem-of-Measurement-and-Sensitivity.pdf)

### Inferences
- Imagery and preparation evidence (Pfordresher & Halpern; the 2025 subvocal paper; Killian & Henry) all points the same way: accurate singers form a clear internal target **before** they phonate. A short, explicit "listen, hear it in your head, sing" rhythm is a low-risk way to train that. It already matches Note by Note's no-headphones call-and-response mode.
- Zarate 2010 suggests that listen-and-identify ear training (Functional Ear Trainer style multiple choice) will not by itself improve singing. Aural exercises in a singing app should end in **sung** responses scored by the pitch detector.
- Interval compression by poor-pitch singers (Pfordresher & Brown) is a specific, measurable signature. The app could detect it from existing data by comparing sung interval sizes with target intervals.

### Gaps
- No randomized study was found that compares "sing immediately after the model" with "silent pause and imagine, then sing" for adult beginners, including the optimal pause length. This is a real gap and should be flagged as untested.
- Sample sizes and effect sizes for the imagery papers were not retrieved.
- Pfordresher & Demorest (2021, *JRME*), "The Prevalence and Correlates of Accurate Singing", is directly relevant, but the full text could not be fetched. — [PDF (blocked)](https://ubwp.buffalo.edu/apal-pfordresher/wp-content/uploads/sites/79/2022/01/PfordresherDemorest_2021_JRME.pdf)

## 4. Interval training: which intervals first, singing vs identifying, songs as anchors, echo/call-and-response and improvising

### Takeaway
Kodály pedagogy starts with **so–mi** (a falling minor third), adds **la**, then **do** and **re** to complete the pentatonic scale, and brings in the semitone degrees **fa and ti** last because semitones are hard to sing. This is a pedagogical tradition built for children, not an experimentally validated order for adults. Empirically, larger intervals produce larger errors in untrained singers, and poor singers compress intervals. Evidence favours training with **sung, echo-style responses**, and one current line of research (Pfordresher & Greenspon 2025) asks whether training across a **wider** pitch range helps. Using songs as interval anchors is common advice with no evidence found.

### Cited Findings
- [Pedagogy] Kodály instruction usually begins with the descending minor third **so–mi**, adds **la**, and builds to the pentatonic scale (do re mi so la). **Fa and ti** come later because the minor second (fa–mi, ti–do) is especially hard for young singers. The general sequence is rhythm before melody, aural before visual, simple before complex. — [Doremi Connect](https://doremiconnect.co.uk/solfa-start-kodaly-music-lessons/); [Teaching Children Music](https://www.teaching-children-music.com/2011/01/kodaly-method/)
- [Pedagogy] The pentatonic emphasis is justified by its presence in folk songs across cultures ("the native musical tongue"). — [Doremi Connect](https://doremiconnect.co.uk/solfa-start-kodaly-music-lessons/)
- [Empirical] Across studies of untrained, amateur and professional singers, sung-interval errors ranged from about 10 to 200 cents, with **untrained singers erring much more on larger intervals** (literature review in PMC5936900; snippet). — [PMC5936900](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5936900/)
- [Empirical] Poor-pitch singers compress interval sizes (Pfordresher & Brown, 2007). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/237377955_Poor-Pitch_Singing_in_the_Absence_of_Tone_Deafness)
- [Empirical, current] Pfordresher & Greenspon (2025, *Musicae Scientiae* 29: 240–255) note that poor singers restrict their pitch range during matching tasks in ways not explained by physical limits. They trained one group to match pitches across a full **octave** and another across a narrower **perfect fifth** (7 semitones), testing whether a wide range better remediates poor singing. **The outcome was not retrieved** (fetch blocked). — [Musicae Scientiae 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10298649241289542)
- [Empirical] Matching is more accurate when the model interval is **sung** than when it is played on piano (snippet). — [PMC5936900](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5936900/)
- [Empirical, children] Guilbault (2004): root-melody accompaniment improved the tonal quality of children's **improvisations**. This is some support for combining harmonic grounding with improvising. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/258156020_The_Effect_of_Harmonic_Accompaniment_on_the_Tonal_Achievement_and_Tonal_Improvisations_of_Children_in_Kindergarten_and_First_Grade)
- [Pedagogy] Gordon's sequence moves from imitation (echoing tonal patterns) to audiation, and later to creativity and improvisation. Patterns are the unit of learning, not isolated intervals. — [Wikipedia: Gordon MLT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_music_learning_theory); [MLT intro PDF](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/56b3c0ca0442625622db7f37/t/5b3575df70a6adda8ddb56f4/1530230241131/MLT+Intro.pdf)
- [App/Marketing] Functional Ear Trainer argues that recognizing a tone's **function in a key** is more transferable than memorizing isolated intervals. — [AdvancingMusician](https://advancingmusician.com/functional-ear-trainer-app/)

### Inferences
- Note by Note's Pitch unit (steps, five-note scale, full scale, leaps, arpeggios, minor, echo) introduces semitones (mi–fa) in the very first step lesson. A Kodály-informed order would be: so–mi, then so–la–so–mi, then mi–re–do, then full pentatonic, then fa and ti. This is untested for adults, but it costs little and front-loads the intervals beginners find easiest.
- The song library already maps onto tonesets and anchors (my own analysis of the melodies, not a cited source):
  - **Mary Had a Little Lamb** uses do–re–mi plus so. It is ideal right after the step lessons.
  - **Twinkle Twinkle** opens with do–do–so–so, a rising perfect fifth.
  - **Amazing Grace** is major pentatonic and opens low so to do, a rising fourth.
  - **Ode to Joy** is mostly stepwise and includes the mi–fa semitone.
  - **Happy Birthday** contains an octave leap ("happy BIRTH-day dear…"), so it belongs late.

  Ordering and tagging songs by toneset and hardest interval, and cross-linking "this leap is the start of Twinkle", uses anchors without claiming evidence for them.
- Identification-only drills are weakly justified for a singing app (see Zarate 2010 in Q3). Echo patterns (2–4 notes) and "sing a different ending that lands on do" improvisation prompts fit the Gordon and Kodály traditions and keep the singer producing sound.

### Gaps
- No empirical study was found testing the Kodály so–mi-first order with **adults**, or testing whether songs used as interval anchors improve interval singing (common advice only; anecdote).
- The Pfordresher & Greenspon (2025) range-training result is unknown. Someone with SAGE access should check it; it bears directly on whether exercises should stay within a fifth or span the singer's octave.
- The "Sing and Clap" and interval-order literature on adult learners was not reached within the tool budget.

## 5. Rhythm and timing for singers: onset timing, pulse, counting-in, subdivision; how apps score timing; rhythm vs pitch for beginners

### Takeaway
In the general population, **timing errors are less frequent than pitch errors**. **Slowing the tempo** lets most occasional singers sing as accurately as professionals. Pulse-keeping behaviours (physically keeping the beat) go with better sight-singing. Kodály and Gordon teach rhythm and pulse early and separately. For adult beginners, pitch is the main difficulty, but tempo is a lever for pitch accuracy. Timing deserves its own separate, lenient score rather than being folded into the pitch score.

### Cited Findings
- [Empirical] Dalla Bella, Giguère & Peretz (2007, *JASA*), "Singing proficiency in the general population": 70% of occasional singers made fewer than 14 pitch errors and fewer than 7 **time** errors per song, the 2-SD threshold. Only a minority made many pitch errors. When nonmusicians sang the same song at a **slow tempo**, most were as accurate as professional singers. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/6457528_Singing_proficiency_in_the_general_population); [Frontiers SPB summary](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00714/full)
- [Empirical] The Sung Performance Battery (Berkowska & Dalla Bella, 2013) measures pitch and time separately to define poor-pitch singing **phenotypes**, reflecting the view that pitch and timing deficits can dissociate. Only the title and summary were retrieved. — [Frontiers 2013](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00714/full)
- [Empirical] Killian & Henry (2005): high-scoring sight-singers **physically kept the beat** significantly more often than low scorers (correlational). — [Killian & Henry 2005](https://doi.org/10.1177/002242940505300105)
- [Pedagogy] Kodály: rhythm before melody. — [Teaching Children Music](https://www.teaching-children-music.com/2011/01/kodaly-method/)
- [Pedagogy] Gordon: rhythm patterns are echoed on a neutral syllable ("bah"). Learners feel macrobeats and microbeats (pulse and subdivision) before reading. Rhythm syllables evolved over the theory's history. — [The Improving Musician](https://theimprovingmusician.com/aural-oral-laying-the-foundation-for-musical-understanding/); [UConn VRME: Evolution of rhythm syllables in MLT](https://digitalcommons.lib.uconn.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1716&context=vrme)

### Inferences
- Beginners will mostly lose points on pitch rather than timing. The practical role of rhythm in Note by Note is (a) a clear **count-in**, including a visual beat, so onsets line up, and (b) **slow default tempos** for new songs with a later "full speed" step. Slow tempo is directly supported by Dalla Bella et al. 2007.
- An app scoring timing should use a separate, generous onset window. Beginners' early or late entries are otherwise likely to be misread as pitch misses: the detector scores the wrong target for the first few hundred milliseconds of the note.
- Beat-keeping movement (tapping a foot, a visual pulse) correlates with better reading. A "tap the beat while you sing" option costs almost nothing, but it is not proven causal. Dalcroze eurhythmics is the tradition built on movement-based rhythm learning, but it was not researched here (see gaps).

### Gaps
- No published description was found of how commercial singing apps (Yousician, Smule, SingStar-type karaoke, EarMaster's singing exercises) set timing windows or weight timing against pitch. This is an open gap. Vendor docs were not reached within the tool budget.
- No evidence was found on the best onset tolerance (in ms) for adult-beginner scoring.
- Dalcroze eurhythmics: no empirical sources were retrieved on its effect on singers' timing.
- Exact sample sizes for Dalla Bella et al. 2007 were not retrieved.

## 6. Reading notation: should a beginner singing app teach staff notation, and how?

### Takeaway
The near-universal teaching consensus (Kodály, Orff, Gordon, Suzuki, Feierabend) is **"sound before symbol"**: build aural and vocal experience first, then attach notation to sounds already known. There is very little direct empirical evidence on notation for adult self-learners, and none was found comparing a relative-pitch "lane" view with staff notation. A reasonable path is to keep the pitch lane as the main view, gradually add scale-degree labels to it, and offer staff notation as an optional later view. Staff notation should not be a gate.

### Cited Findings
- [Pedagogy] "Sound before sight/symbol" is shared by Orff Schulwerk, Kodály, Gordon's Music Learning Theory, Suzuki and Feierabend. Learners first listen, sing, move and play, and notation comes after the meaning has been built through experience. Proponents compare it to learning speech before reading. — [Victoria Boler](https://www.victoriaboler.com/blog/sound-before-sight-in-elementary-general-music); [Colourful Keys](https://colourfulkeys.ie/music-reading-through-rote-teaching/); [Pianodao: lessons from history](https://pianodao.com/2015/10/17/sound-before-symbol-lessons-from-history/)
- [Pedagogy/opinion] Several sound-before-symbol articles invoke neuroscience ("the neuroscientific key"). These are blog claims without specific cited studies and should be treated as marketing or anecdote. — [The Maestro Online](https://www.the-maestro-online.com/blog/why-is-the-sound-before-symbol-approach-the-neuroscientific-key-to-future-music-education/)
- [Pedagogy] Gordon's sequence puts aural/oral imitation and verbal association (naming patterns) **before** symbolic association (notation). — [Wikipedia: Gordon MLT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_music_learning_theory); [GIML](https://giml.org/mlt/about/)
- [Empirical, correlational] In Demorest & May (1995), years of **piano** study were the second-strongest predictor of choristers' sight-singing, after years of school choir. — [Demorest & May 1995](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.2307/3345676)
- [Empirical, current] Biaggi (2026) reviews the literature on music-reading fluency for beginning children at the piano (*Update*). Its conclusions were not retrieved, but it is the most recent review found. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/87551233261473259)
- [Empirical] An online study of how teaching method affects music-reading skills and music participation exists. Its findings were not retrieved. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/305617694_The_Effect_of_Music_Teaching_Method_on_Music_Reading_Skills_and_Music_Participation_An_Online_Study)

### Inferences
- For an adult whose goal is to sing songs, staff notation is a separate skill. The traditions say it should come after solid aural and vocal work, not before. Note by Note's scrolling pitch lane is already a relative, proportional "iconic" notation (height = pitch, length = duration). It matches the sound-before-symbol stage better than a staff does.
- A gradual bridge that fits the traditions:
  1. Pitch lane with no labels.
  2. Lane notes labelled with movable-do or scale-degree numbers.
  3. A "reading" lesson where the lane is shown **before** audio plays and the learner sings it cold. This is sight-singing on the lane.
  4. An optional staff view of the same line in the transposed key, with the lane still visible.

  Transposing to the singer's key makes the staff view's key signatures vary. That is a further reason to keep the staff optional and label notes by function.

### Gaps
- No empirical study was found comparing pitch-lane or contour displays with staff notation for learning to sing or for later reading transfer.
- No adult self-learner data was found on whether learning notation increases retention or motivation in singing apps.

## Implications for a self-guided singing app

Each item gives the change for Note by Note and the evidence strength behind it. Items are ordered by how directly they rest on evidence first, then by cost.

1. **Establish the key before every exercise, and have the singer sing "do" first.** Play a short cadence (I–IV–V–I) or the tonic triad, then ask for one sung tonic before the pattern starts. Score that "do" silently for a calibration check.
   - *Evidence:* tonal context improves interval perception and melody memory (Graves & Oxenham 2017; Saffran 2003; Groulx 2013, all empirical). Good sight-singers tonicize (Killian & Henry 2005, empirical, correlational). Functional Ear Trainer uses the same cadence-first design (app rationale).
2. **Offer a tonic (or tonic + fifth) drone as an optional headphones-only mode. Do not promise it improves accuracy, and alternate drone-on and drone-off repetitions.** Keep scoring on unaccompanied repetitions as the true measure.
   - *Evidence:* three controlled studies found no measured intonation benefit from drones or accompaniment (Springer et al. 2021; Zabanal 2019; Atterbury & Silcox 1993 / Kuhn & Sims 1983 in children), but users strongly prefer drones (survey data in Springer et al. 2021). The evidence is strong that drones don't hurt and weak that they help.
3. **Make the guide tone more voice-like** (a vowel-like sample or formant-filtered tone), or offer a "voice" timbre option alongside piano, for match-a-note and echo lessons.
   - *Evidence:* a live voice model improved pitch matching (J Voice 2013, empirical; details not retrieved). Sung models are imitated more accurately than piano models (PMC5936900 review). Moderate evidence, mostly from children and small samples.
4. **Tolerate just-intonation deviations in scoring and feedback.** When a drone or chord is sounding, don't flag mi (the major third) or la (the major sixth) as "flat" for deviations under about 15 cents. Consider reporting flat/sharp tendency per scale degree rather than overall, so a JI-leaning flat third isn't read as a general flat habit.
   - *Evidence:* singers lean toward just intonation (Howard 2007, empirical, small sample). The 14-cent ET/JI third difference is arithmetic.
5. **Build an explicit "Listen, then think it, then sing" step into call-and-response mode.** After the model, show a 1–2 beat silent "hear it in your head" countdown before the sing cue. Add an inner-hearing song game: the piano plays a known song, drops out for a bar, and the singer must come back in on the right pitch. This uses the existing songs.
   - *Evidence:* poor imagery goes with poor imitation (Pfordresher & Halpern 2013, empirical, correlational). Preparation time improves sight-singing (Killian & Henry 2005). Gordon's audiation and Kodály's inner hearing are pedagogy. Causal evidence for a silent-pause step is **untested**, so A/B-test it with the app's own score data.
6. **Make every aural exercise end in singing, not multiple-choice.** If scale-degree ear training is added (Functional Ear Trainer style), the response should be "sing it back, then sing down to do", scored by the pitch detector.
   - *Evidence:* discrimination training alone did not improve vocal accuracy (Zarate et al. 2010, empirical, not re-verified here). Current theory treats poor-pitch singing as a sensorimotor mapping problem (Pfordresher & Brown 2007; Hutchins et al. 2014).
7. **Reorder or add a pentatonic path in the Pitch unit:** so–mi, then so–la–so–mi, then mi–re–do, then full pentatonic, with semitones (mi–fa, ti–do) later, before the full-scale lesson. Keep the existing "steps" lesson but move semitone steps after the pentatonic lessons.
   - *Evidence:* Kodály sequence (pedagogy, designed for children). Untrained singers err more on larger intervals (empirical review). Semitone difficulty is pedagogical lore, not adult data.
8. **Use the singer's full comfortable octave in match-a-note and leap exercises,** not just a narrow band around the middle.
   - *Evidence:* the theory that poor singers restrict their range beyond physical limits, and the design of Pfordresher & Greenspon 2025 (empirical; **outcome not retrieved**, so treat as a hypothesis until checked).
9. **Detect and report interval compression.** In results and in "Copy results for Claude", compute sung interval size divided by target interval size for leaps, e.g. "your leaps were on average 80% of their true size". Also report per-scale-degree tendencies ("ti sharp, fa flat"), not only overall cents and flat/sharp.
   - *Evidence:* interval compression is the signature of poor-pitch singers (Pfordresher & Brown 2007, empirical). It gives the AI coach something specific to act on.
10. **Add optional scale-degree labels on the pitch lane,** chosen by the user as off, numbers (1–7) or movable-do (do re mi…). Never use letter names, because lessons transpose. Default to off or neutral "loo" for Unit 1, and prompt to turn labels on in the Pitch unit.
    - *Evidence:* no system is proven superior (Killian & Henry 2005; Demorest & May 1995 confounded; USF dissertation contested). Movable-do is dominant in practice (survey). Letter names did worse in some child studies (Frey-Clark 2017 review). Gordon uses neutral syllables first (pedagogy).
11. **Skip Curwen hand signs as a core feature.** At most, show a small hand-sign illustration as optional flavour.
    - *Evidence:* mixed or null results in controlled child studies (Boisvert, n = 66; Frey-Clark review). There is no evidence at all for solo phone users.
12. **Add an echo-pattern game and an "answer" improvisation game.** Short 2–4 note tonal patterns on a neutral syllable, echoed back. Then "the app sings a question; you sing any answer that ends on do", scored only for ending on the tonic and staying in key.
    - *Evidence:* Gordon's aural/oral stage and Kodály echo singing (pedagogy). Harmonic grounding improved the tonality of children's improvisations (Guilbault 2004, empirical, children). This extends the existing echo game.
13. **Timing: add a visual count-in and beat, slow tempos for new songs, and a separate lenient timing score.** Song lessons should start at a slow tempo with a "full speed" unlock. Show timing as its own result line ("entries: mostly on time / often late") with a generous onset window, and do not count the first ~200 ms of each note against pitch. Optionally offer a "tap the beat" mode.
    - *Evidence:* slow tempo lets occasional singers match professionals (Dalla Bella et al. 2007, empirical). Timing errors are rarer than pitch errors in the general population (same study). Beat-keeping goes with better sight-singing (Killian & Henry 2005, correlational). The exact onset-window values are **not evidence-based** (gap) and must be tuned empirically.
14. **Order and tag songs by toneset and hardest interval, and use them as interval anchors.** Suggested order: Mary Had a Little Lamb (do re mi so), then Are You Sleeping / Row Row Row / Ode to Joy (stepwise, introduces fa), then Twinkle (opening perfect fifth) and Amazing Grace (pentatonic, opening perfect fourth), then Waltzing Matilda, then Happy Birthday (octave leap) last. When a leap lesson uses a fifth, say "this is the start of Twinkle".
    - *Evidence:* Kodály sequencing of tonesets and pentatonic folk material (pedagogy). Untrained singers err more on large intervals (empirical). The anchor effect itself is **anecdotal**, with no study found.
15. **Keep the pitch lane as the main notation, and add a reading bridge rather than a staff-first lesson.** Add a "preview then sing cold" mode (sight-singing on the lane), then an optional staff view mirrored under the lane for curious users.
    - *Evidence:* "sound before symbol" consensus across Kodály, Orff, Gordon and Suzuki (pedagogy). There is no empirical comparison of lane vs staff (gap). Transposition makes staff key signatures vary per user.
16. **Add a motivating framing message in "Start here".** For example: "Most adults who think they can't sing in tune actually can; in lab tests about 85–90% of non-singers sing in tune. What improves it is practice matching and hearing notes in your head."
    - *Evidence:* Dalla Bella and colleagues (empirical): 85–90% sing in tune, while about 60% of students believe they can't.
