# Motor learning, practice structure and motivation for adult beginners learning to sing on their own

Scope: evidence-based principles for session design, progression, review and motivation in a self-guided singing app (context: "Note by Note", a free PWA used by an adult beginner on iPhone). Research current to 30 September 2026.

Evidence labels used throughout:
- **[A]** peer-reviewed empirical evidence (study, year, sample, effect where available)
- **[B]** established pedagogical consensus or tradition, or theoretical frameworks
- **[C]** marketing, company-reported data, blogs, or anecdote

Method note: many publisher and repository domains (frontiersin.org, nature.com, pubmed/pmc, buffalo.edu, blog.duolingo.com, unlv.edu) were blocked by the network proxy in this session, so a lot of findings below rest on search-result abstracts and snippets, not full texts. Where I relied on my own knowledge of a paper's content rather than a retrieved text, I say so. Effect sizes are given only where a retrieved source stated them.

## 1. Spaced vs massed, interleaved vs blocked, variability, contextual interference and deliberate practice: what is music- or singing-specific and what transfers from motor learning?

### Takeaway
General motor-learning evidence supports spacing sessions across days (sleep consolidation), mixing practice items once the basics are in place, and judging progress on **delayed retention** rather than in-session performance. Music-specific studies back up the spacing and consolidation findings but are small and mostly use trained instrumentalists. **No controlled study of practice scheduling with adult beginner singers was found.** The "10,000 hours / deliberate practice" account (Ericsson et al. 1993) has been substantially weakened by replication and meta-analysis. How practice is done (finding and fixing errors) matters more than how much is done.

### Cited Findings
**Framework (motor learning to speech)**
- [A/B] Maas, Robin, Hula, Freedman, Wulf, Ballard & Schmidt (2008, *AJSLP* 17(3):277–298) is the standard tutorial applying motor-learning principles to speech. It groups them into pre-practice, principles of practice and principles of feedback, and reviews how far non-speech findings carry over to speech motor learning. — [ASHA](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1058-0360(2008/025)); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Principles-of-motor-learning-in-treatment-of-motor-Maas-Robin/49f5060b40cb4980e58e712e874497a1c33a3762). *(Full text could not be fetched here. From my own knowledge of the paper: it covers practice amount, distribution, variability, schedule (blocked vs random), attentional focus, feedback type (knowledge of performance vs knowledge of results), feedback frequency and timing. Its central point is that conditions which improve performance during practice can differ from those that improve learning, meaning retention and transfer. It also says many principles have little direct evidence in speech. Treat these details as unverified in this session.)*
- [B] Challenge Point Framework (Guadagnoli & Lee 2004, *J Motor Behavior* 36:212–224): the learning value of a practice condition depends on **functional** difficulty, which is task difficulty relative to the learner's skill and the conditions. Harder conditions such as random practice raise learning potential but lower practice performance, and there is an optimal point for each learner and task. The implication is that beginners need lower contextual interference than experts. — [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Challenge_point_framework); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/8574634_Challenge_Point_A_Framework_for_Conceptualizing_the_Effects_of_Various_Practice_Conditions_in_Motor_Learning); a 2025 scoping review of CPF applications exists — [Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00222895.2025.2508283)

**Contextual interference (CI) / interleaving: general motor learning**
- [A] Czyż, Wójcik, Solarská & Kiper (2024, *Scientific Reports*): systematic review and meta-analysis. From 1,255 records, 294 full texts were screened and 54 studies included. Under both a three-level model and a random-effects model, **high CI (random/interleaved) had a medium beneficial effect on retention**. — [Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-65753-3); [NWU repository](https://repository.nwu.ac.za/items/c9dff204-b9df-4d71-af83-c232f2dc8c97)
- [A] A companion 2024 meta-analysis on **transfer** found a medium effect favouring random practice (**SMD = 0.55**). — [PubMed 39205981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39205981/)
- [A, contested] These meta-analyses were formally criticised (a SportRxiv comment) and answered in *Educational Psychology Review* (2025). CI remains debated, especially for applied settings versus lab tasks. — [SportRxiv comment](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/435); [Educ Psychol Rev 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10043-1)

**Contextual interference: music-specific**
- [A] Carter & Grahn (2016, *Frontiers in Psychology* 7:1251): **10 advanced clarinettists**. Two pieces were practised blocked (12 min each) and two interleaved (3-min chunks alternated to 12 min each). Performances were recorded after practice and **one day later** and rated by three professional clarinettists. Where ratings differed, interleaved pieces were rated better, but results varied across raters and effects were "modest". Participants rated interleaving as more useful (for goal setting, focus and mistake identification) but still **preferred** blocked practice, which the authors attribute to the feeling of fluency that repetition gives. — [Grahn lab summary](https://www.jessicagrahn.com/cartergrahn-2016.html); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27588014/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/306264792_Optimizing_Music_Learning_Exploring_How_Blocked_and_Interleaved_Practice_Schedules_Affect_Advanced_Performance)
- [A] Mathias & Goldman (2025, *Journal of Research in Music Education*): **20 advanced violinists** compared blocked, random and an **increasing-CI** schedule (blocked early, then progressively more interleaved), the schedule sport research has found advantageous. The results could not be retrieved in this session. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00224294231222801)

**Spacing, sleep and consolidation: music-specific**
- [A] Simmons (2012, *JRME*): **29 non-pianist musicians** learned a 9-note keyboard sequence over three practice sessions separated by 5 min, 6 h or 24 h. Accuracy improved significantly **only in the 24-h group**. — [ERIC EJ951332](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ951332); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/258156119_Distributed_Practice_and_Procedural_Memory_Consolidation_in_Musicians'_Skill_Learning)
- [A] Simmons & Duke (2006) sleep study: musicians who were not pianists learned a 12-note melody with the non-dominant hand. Those who **slept between sessions** improved accuracy significantly, and a same-day retest group did not. — [UT Center for Music Learning](https://cml.music.utexas.edu/research/skill-learning-and-procedural-memory); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249808789_Procedural_Memory_Consolidation_in_the_Performance_of_Brief_Keyboard_Sequences)
- [A] Allen & Duke (2013, *Update*): limited, restricted practice still produced overnight memory enhancement in music learning. — [SAGE](https://doi.org/10.1177/8755123313502350)
- [A] Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006, *Psychological Bulletin* 132:354–380): meta-analysis of 839 assessments from 317 experiments (verbal recall). Spacing beats massing, and **the optimal gap between sessions grows as the desired retention interval grows**. Few studies tested retention beyond a day or a week. — [York U](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/CPVWR2006.html); [PDF](https://augmentingcognition.com/assets/Cepeda2006.pdf)

**How practice is done beats how much**
- [A] Duke, Simmons & Cash (2009, *JRME*, "It's Not How Much; It's How"): **17 advanced pianists** practised a hard 3-bar passage, with a retention test the next day. **Practice time and number of trials were not related to retention ranking.** The best performers made fewer errors, **addressed errors immediately**, found the precise source of each error, varied the tempo of problem segments systematically, and repeated targets until they were fixed. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429408328851); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249808857_It's_Not_How_Much_It's_How_Characteristics_of_Practice_Behavior_and_Retention_of_Performance_Skills)

**Deliberate practice: the older finding has been superseded**
- [A] Macnamara & Maitra (2019, *Royal Society Open Science*): replication of Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993) with **39 violinists** (3 groups of 13) using diaries and interviews. Deliberate practice explained about **26%** of variance, against 48% originally. The "good" group had logged *more* deliberate practice (≈9,844 h) than the "best" group (≈8,224 h), a non-significant difference. The popular "10,000-hour rule" should be treated as superseded. — [Royal Society Open Science](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rsos.190327); [PDF](https://artscimedia.case.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/141/2019/10/03143050/Macnamara-Maitra-2019.pdf). A rebuttal from the Ericsson side argues the definition of deliberate practice was misapplied — [PMC8049893](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8049893/)

**Attentional focus (relevant to how instructions are worded)**
- [A] Chua, Jimenez-Diaz, Lewthwaite, Kim & Wulf (2021, *Psychological Bulletin* 147:618–645): meta-analyses covering 73 studies (1,824 participants) for performance and 40 studies (1,274) for learning. An **external focus** (on the effect of a movement) beat an internal focus (on body parts): performance g = 0.26, retention g = 0.58, with no moderation by age or skill level. *Caveat: written by the originating lab. The retrieved snippet did not cover publication-bias checks. The same field's self-control and expectancy literatures were later shown to be inflated by bias (see Q4), so treat the size as an upper bound.* — [PubMed 34843301](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34843301/); [Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/superiority-of-external-attentional-focus-for-motor-performance-a/)
- [A] Singing-specific: Atkins & Duke (2013, *International Journal of Research in Choral Singing*), **untrained singers**. Expert-rated tone quality was **higher when singers directed the sound to fingers on the nose, a microphone or a point on the far wall than when they focused on throat vibrations**. Atkins (2017, *JRME*), 20 trained singers: better tone when aiming sound at the back wall or "filling the room" than with internal cues (soft palate, vibrato). — [Atkins & Duke PDF](https://acda-publications.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/IJRCS/volumefour/ijrcs4_2_atkinsduke.pdf); [Atkins 2017, SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0022429416673842)

**Variability of practice: range (singing-specific)**
- [A] Pfordresher & Greenspon (2025, *Musicae Scientiae* 29:240–255): pitch-matching training for inaccurate singers. One group matched pitches across **one octave** and the other across **seven semitones**, and there was a control group. **Only the wide-range group improved significantly**, and only for single-pitch matching, not four-note melodies. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10298649241289542)

### Inferences
- Of the practice-structure principles, spacing across days with sleep in between has the most direct music evidence, from small samples of trained musicians on keyboard tasks. It probably transfers to vocal pitch-motor mapping, but no singing study was found that tests it.
- Under the challenge-point view, CI should be **low at first and rising** for novices: blocked introduction of a new exercise, then mixed review once it is basically stable. The increasing-CI schedule tested by Mathias & Goldman (2025) matches this idea, but its results were not retrieved.
- Learners will prefer blocked, repetitive practice because it feels fluent (Carter & Grahn). An app that only reports the score from the end of a lesson will reinforce an illusion of learning. Next-day retests are a better signal.
- Pfordresher & Greenspon (2025) is unusually direct evidence for **variability** in singing: sampling targets from a wider range helped single-note matching.

### Gaps
- No RCTs of blocked vs interleaved or spaced vs massed schedules with **adult beginner singers**. All music CI studies found use advanced instrumentalists.
- Mathias & Goldman (2025) results could not be retrieved.
- The Maas et al. (2008) text and Wulf's OPTIMAL-theory claims could not be re-read in full here.

## 2. Optimal session length and frequency for adult beginner singers; vocal load vs learning; value of short daily sessions

### Takeaway
No empirical study defines an optimal session length for adult beginner singers. Pedagogical convention ([B]/[C]) is **15–20 minutes, building to 30–45 minutes**, with short frequent sessions preferred. Voice science ([A]) shows that about **30 minutes of loaded voice use measurably raises perceived effort and fatigue** in some protocols, but no safe dose threshold has been established. Spacing and sleep research (Q1) supports **short, daily or near-daily sessions** over occasional long ones, and Duke et al. (2009) show that more minutes do not by themselves predict retention.

### Cited Findings
- [B/C] Voice-teacher guidance, mostly from studio blogs: start with **15–20 min** sessions and build to 30–45 min as stamina grows. Several shorter sessions beat one long one. Warm-ups beyond 15–20 min risk fatigue before the real practice starts. — [VoiceLessons.com](https://www.voicelessons.com/blog/how-long-should-you-do-vocal-exercises-guide); [The Piano Place](https://thepianoplaceutah.com/optimizing-your-vocal-practice-how-long-should-i-practice-singing-each-day/); [VoiceLessons.com burnout](https://www.voicelessons.com/blog/educational/how-to-practice-singing-every-day-without-burnout). *These are commercial studio sources, not research. They reflect common teaching practice but cite no evidence.*
- [A/B] National Center for Voice and Speech (NCVS): dosimetry can measure time dose (accumulated seconds of vocal-fold vibration), cycle dose and distance dose, but **no threshold of safe voice use has been established**. Students are often left to pace themselves without understanding their limits or the value of planned rest. — [NCVS](https://ncvs.org/strategies-for-safety-thresholds-of-phonation-for-performers-via-dosimetry/)
- [A] A musical-theatre rehearsal dosimetry study (*Journal of Voice*, 2023) found actual phonation (time dose) took up only **4–7% of rehearsal time**. Even in singing-heavy rehearsals the voice is vibrating for a small fraction of the clock time. — [J Voice](https://www.jvoice.org/article/S0892-1997(23)00344-2/abstract)
- [A] Amateur karaoke singers: a group given water and rest breaks sang significantly longer before fatigue (mean ≈102 min) than a group without them (≈85 min) (*Journal of Voice*, 2003). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199703000389)
- [A] Vocal-fatigue research: loading tasks most commonly last about 2 h (range 15 min to 3.75 h). In some protocols a **30-minute task requiring changed vocal parameters significantly raised perceived effort and fatigue**. — [Scoping review, J Voice 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199724000250); [Dose-based vocal loading task, PMC7055723](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7055723/)
- [B] Voice rest guidance: people with very heavy vocal demands may set aside a rest day a week. Absolute voice rest is rarely needed for more than about a week. — [NATS Journal of Singing, Sataloff et al. 2019](https://www.nats.org/_Library/JOS_On_Point/JOS-075-5-2019-557_-_Voice_Rest_-_Satalof_-_Cline_-_Lyons_-_Skeffington_-_Rubin.pdf); [St Olaf Musician Health](https://wp.stolaf.edu/musician-health/resting-your-voice/)
- [A] Spacing and consolidation: only 24-h spacing improved keyboard-sequence accuracy (Simmons 2012). Sleep between sessions produced gains (Simmons & Duke 2006). Practice time did not predict retention (Duke et al. 2009). — see Q1 citations: [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ951332); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429408328851)
- [A] Visual-feedback singing training: Pfordresher's lab reports that **even a single ~20-minute session** with augmented visual feedback can improve singing accuracy. — [Berglin, Pfordresher & Demorest 2022, SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/03057356211026730) *(from the search-result summary; not verified against the full text)*
- [C] Duolingo reports that learners who "binge" lessons were much more likely to abandon the app than those who paced themselves. — as reported in [Yu-kai Chou summary](https://yukaichou.com/gamification-study/master-the-art-of-streak-design-for-short-term-engagement-and-long-term-success/) and [StriveCloud](https://www.strivecloud.io/blog/gamification-examples-boost-user-retention-duolingo) (the original Duolingo blog was blocked). This is company data and correlational: bingers may differ from pacers in other ways.

### Inferences
- A **10–20 minute** daily session, for example a 3-min warm-up, one core lesson, a short review block and a song line, sits within both teaching convention and fatigue data. Because actual phonation is a fraction of session time, vocal load for a beginner at normal loudness in such a session is very unlikely to be a problem. Strain from pushing range, or loud or pressed singing, is the more plausible risk.
- An app that uses pitch detection can **measure voiced seconds** as a time-dose proxy, which is the NCVS dosimetry concept. Showing "you sang for 4 min 20 s today" is honest and useful. It should not be framed as a medical limit, because no threshold exists.
- Daily frequency matters mainly because of consolidation between sessions. **Two or three sessions on different days probably beat one long session a week**, as an inference from Simmons (2012) and Cepeda (2006).

### Gaps
- No empirical data on the best session length or weekly frequency for adult novice singers.
- No established safe vocal dose. Fatigue studies mostly use speech or trained singers, and loud or long loading tasks.

## 3. Adaptive difficulty and mastery-based progression: when to advance, streaks of success, spaced review, and adapting tolerance, tempo, range and interval size

### Takeaway
Theory ([B]) and meta-analysis ([A]) support **mastery gating**, meaning advancement only after a criterion is met, with remediation otherwise, and **keeping difficulty near an optimal challenge point** that shifts as skill grows. The "85% rule" is a theoretical result from machine learning and is **not validated for motor or singing skill**, but it fits the challenge-point idea and is a reasonable starting value. Spaced-review models such as Duolingo's half-life regression are well developed for verbal items. No singing app has published data on thresholds. **Specific cents tolerances and success criteria for beginners are not settled by evidence** and should be set, then tuned from users' own data.

### Cited Findings
- [A] Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990, *Review of Educational Research* 60:265–299): 108 controlled evaluations. Mastery learning raised exam scores by **ES ≈ 0.52 SD**, with **larger effects for weaker students** (roughly 50th to 70th percentile). Group-based programmes beat self-paced ones. Effects were ≈0.4 SD on locally made criterion tests and ≈0.1 SD on standardised tests. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543060002265); [ERIC EJ415887](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ415887); [PDF](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/kulik_kulik_Bangert-Drowns_1990.pdf). *The effects are from classroom academic learning, and the finding that self-paced programmes had smaller effects matters for a self-guided app. The specific mastery threshold (such as 80%) was not confirmed in retrieved text.*
- [B] Challenge Point Framework (Guadagnoli & Lee 2004): the optimal challenge point maximises learning while limiting the loss of performance during practice. It **shifts upward as skill improves**, which is the basis for adaptive difficulty. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Challenge_point_framework); [ScienceDirect: measuring functional task difficulty](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167945715300105)
- [A, theoretical] Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia & Cohen (2019, *Nature Communications* 10:4646), "The Eighty Five Percent Rule": for gradient-descent-type learners on **binary classification**, learning is fastest at a training error rate of about 15.87%, or roughly 85% accuracy. It was shown in models and simulations, not in human motor learning. — [Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-12552-4); [Princeton](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/the-eighty-five-percent-rule-for-optimal-learning/). *Popular write-ups overgeneralise it (e.g., [Medium](https://mindsetrobert.medium.com/the-rule-of-85-for-optimal-learning-and-the-importance-of-failure-d290a84a37d0)) [C].*
- [A] Settles & Meeder (2016, ACL), Duolingo's half-life regression (HLR): a trainable spaced-repetition model that estimates each item's memory half-life. It reduced recall-prediction error by **45%+ versus baselines** and improved **daily engagement by 12%** in an operational user study. Code and data are public. — [ACL PDF](https://aclanthology.org/anthology-files/pdf/P/P16/P16-1174.pdf); [GitHub](https://github.com/duolingo/halflife-regression). *Built for vocabulary, not motor skills. The engagement figure is Duolingo-internal.*
- [A] Spacing research (Cepeda 2006) indicates that the best interval between reviews grows with the desired retention horizon, which supports expanding review intervals. — [York U](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/CPVWR2006.html)
- [A] Range: training across a **wider (octave) pitch range** helped single-pitch matching more than a 7-semitone range (Pfordresher & Greenspon 2025). — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10298649241289542)
- [A] Tempo: expert practicers **varied the tempo of problem segments systematically** and repeated them until the errors were fixed (Duke et al. 2009). — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429408328851)
- [A] Poor-pitch prevalence and definitions: roughly **10–15%** of adults are measurably inaccurate singers (Pfordresher & Brown 2007). Pfordresher et al. (2010, *JASA*, "Imprecise singing is widespread") distinguishes accuracy (mean deviation from target) from precision (consistency). — [Pfordresher & Brown 2007 PDF](http://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf); [Pfordresher et al. 2010 PDF](http://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher_etal_2010_JASA.pdf) *(PDFs blocked in this session; details from search summaries)*

### Inferences
- Advance on **delayed** evidence, not only same-session evidence. For example, a skill counts as passed when about 80–85% of target notes fall inside the current tolerance **on two sessions on different days**. This combines mastery learning (Kulik), the performance-vs-learning distinction (Maas) and consolidation (Simmons). The numbers are a design starting point, not an evidence-derived constant.
- Keep the success rate near the challenge point, with **several independent dials**: cents tolerance (tighten as the user succeeds), tempo (slow a failing segment, speed up after success), interval size (steps, then small leaps, then larger leaps), and range (within the comfortable range at first, then gradually widen towards the octave-wide sampling that helped in Pfordresher & Greenspon 2025). Change **one dial at a time** so a failure can be traced to one cause. This is pedagogical logic ([B]), not a tested algorithm.
- Show both accuracy and precision (mean miss in cents and spread). The app already shows average miss and flat/sharp tendency. A consistency measure would separate "consistently flat", which is fixable by recalibrating, from "scattered", which needs more basic pitch-matching work.
- Spaced review can be simple, for example re-testing a passed exercise at roughly 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days, stretching the interval after a pass and shrinking it after a miss. An HLR-style model is only worth it with far more users.

### Gaps
- No published thresholds (cents tolerance, success percentage, number of successful repetitions) validated for advancing adult beginner singers.
- No study found testing adaptive-difficulty algorithms in singing apps.
- The 85% rule is untested for continuous-output motor skills such as pitch production.

## 4. Adult learner motivation and self-regulated learning: SDT, goals, self-efficacy, singing anxiety and "I can't sing" identity. What helps adults who believe they cannot sing?

### Takeaway
Self-labelled "non-singers" and the "tone deaf" are far more common than real impairment. About **17%** of Western adults self-define as tone deaf, and about **60%** of students in one large sample said they cannot imitate melodies, yet only about **10–15%** sing measurably inaccurately. Singing accuracy is **trainable in adults**, including through visual-feedback training and short structured programmes. The "non-singer" identity is usually rooted in negative childhood experiences and social judgement. It can be changed through positive, low-judgement singing experiences. Self-determination theory (autonomy, competence, relatedness) is the dominant motivation framework in music education. Several popular motor-learning motivational claims (choice or "self-controlled practice" and "enhanced expectancies" boosting *learning*) have been **substantially weakened** by bias-corrected meta-analyses.

### Cited Findings
**Self-labelling vs measured ability**
- [A] Sloboda, Wise & Peretz (2005, *Annals NYAS* 1060:255–261): the general public mostly uses "tone deaf" to mean **perceived poor singing**, not a perceptual deficit. About **17%** of Western adults self-define as tone deaf, and most of them are not congenitally amusic. — [Wiley](https://nyaspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1196/annals.1360.018); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7181556_Quantifying_Tone_Deafness_in_the_General_Population)
- [A] Wise & Sloboda (2008, *Musicae Scientiae* 12(1)) profiled self-defined "tone deaf" adults on perception, singing performance and self-assessment. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/102986490801200102)
- [A] Cuddy et al. (2005, *Annals NYAS*): "Musical difficulties are rare". — [Wiley](https://nyaspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1196/annals.1360.026)
- [A] Pfordresher & Brown (2007, *Music Perception*): about **60%** of 1,000+ university students judged they could not accurately imitate melodies, but lab testing found roughly **85–90%** of occasional singers sing in tune. Poor-pitch singing mostly reflects **difficulty mapping perception onto vocal motor commands** rather than a hearing deficit. — [PDF](http://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf) (via search summary); see also [Pfordresher & Demorest 2021, JRME](https://ubwp.buffalo.edu/apal-pfordresher/wp-content/uploads/sites/79/2022/01/PfordresherDemorest_2021_JRME.pdf)
- [A] Pfordresher & Mantell (2014, *Cognitive Psychology*): poor-pitch singers imitate recordings of **their own voice** more accurately than other voices, which supports an inverse-model (sensorimotor mapping) account. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010028514000036)

**Trainability in adults**
- [A] Berglin, Pfordresher & Demorest (2022, *Psychology of Music* 50(4):1077–1090): training for adult poor-pitch singers with visual and auditory feedback. Visual feedback looked more promising than auditory feedback, which has shown **mixed** benefits in earlier studies. The benefit of visual-feedback training was clearer for learners with **low initial accuracy**. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/03057356211026730); detail on the moderation by initial ability from the 2026 review below.
- [A] Finnish intervention study with **10 adult "non-singers"**. All had serious emotional and belief blocks from childhood singing experiences, and most had perceptual and production problems such as narrow range. After a relatively short intervention they made "remarkable" progress, with test scores above the Finnish untrained average. Title quote: "Singing is no longer forbidden to me – it's like part of my human dignity has been restored." — [European Publisher](https://www.europeanpublisher.com/en/article/10.15405/ejsbs.149); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/273300597_'Singing_is_No_Longer_Forbidden_to_Me_-_It's_Like_Part_of_My_Human_Dignity_Has_Been_Restored.'_Adult_Non-Singers_Learning_To_Sing_An_Explorative_Intervention_Study). *Small uncontrolled sample.*
- [A] Mitchell (1991, *Psychology of Music*), research note on adult non-singers in the early stages of learning to sing. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0305735691191006)

**Identity, anxiety and labels**
- [A] Abril (2007, *Music Education Research*), "I have a voice but I just can't sing": a narrative study linking non-singer identity to **social anxiety** and feeling judged. — [Everyday Musicality summary](https://everydaymusicality.com/2019/05/15/adults-anxiety-toward-singing-abril-2007/)
- [A/B] Knight (1999), "Exploring a cultural myth: what adult non-singers may reveal about the nature of singing". Whidden (2009) on the **injustice of singer/non-singer labels** applied by music educators. Whidden (2010): a "non-singer" self-concept **can change to "singer" through positive singing experiences**. Negative childhood experiences at home and school are a major source of the label. — as summarised in [ERIC EJ1269707](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1269707.pdf) and [U Michigan "I Don't Sing" manuscript](https://backend.production.deepblue-documents.lib.umich.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/941c8fc2-b7e2-44f6-9a01-51ce6458e668/content)
- [A] Demorest et al. (2016, *JRME*): singing ability relates to musical self-concept and to intentions to keep making music. — [PDF](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Demorest_etal_2016_JRME.pdf)

**Motivation theory**
- [B] Evans (2015, *Musicae Scientiae* 19:65–83): a self-determination theory approach to music education. Motivational quality depends on meeting the needs for **competence, relatedness and autonomy**, and autonomy-supportive environments improve long-term music motivation. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1029864914568044); [UNSW](https://unsworks.unsw.edu.au/entities/publication/90f9c832-ece0-4d58-ac15-93b37de60870). An empirical follow-up (Evans & Bonneville-Roussy 2016, *Psychology of Music*) examines self-determined motivation in music students — [PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/2016_EvansBonneville-Roussy_SelfDetermined.pdf)
- [A] A 2026 systematic review in *Frontiers in Psychology*, "Acoustic spectral feedback in vocal learning", combines motor-learning theory with **self-regulated learning** (Zimmerman's three-phase cycle), metacognition and **Bandura's self-efficacy**. It argues that vocal learning is "invisible", so learners working alone struggle to detect and correct errors, and that visual pitch displays make the process visible. Its integrated pathway model is explicitly an **interpretive proposition, not a verified mechanism**. — [Frontiers 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1920074/full); [PubMed 42694130](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42694130/)

**Superseded or contested motor-learning motivation claims**
- [A] McKay et al. (2022, *Meta-Psychology* 6): meta-analysis of self-controlled (learner-choice) motor practice. Qualitative reviews suggested a clear benefit, but after correcting for selective reporting the **benefit is small, and p-curve suggested a lack of evidential value**. — [Meta-Psychology](https://open.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/article/view/2803)
- [A] McKay et al. (2025, *International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology* 18(1)): "reporting bias and underpowered study designs has **substantially exaggerated** the motor learning benefits of self-controlled practice **and enhanced expectancies**". This undercuts two pillars of Wulf & Lewthwaite's OPTIMAL theory. — [Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1750984X.2023.2207255)
- [A] A 2025 meta-analysis on self-controlled feedback found positive published effects but **insufficient evidence that it beats yoked practice**. — [PMC12467369](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12467369/)

### Inferences
- For a user who believes they "can't sing", the strongest evidence-based message is factual and normalising. Most people who think this sing reasonably accurately when tested, and those who do not are mostly dealing with a trainable sensorimotor mapping problem, not a hearing defect. The app's on-device processing with nothing recorded directly addresses the fear of judgement described by Abril (2007).
- **Early experiences of competence** matter most for identity change (Whidden). The first two sessions should be designed so the user succeeds: generous tolerance, notes in the middle of their measured range, and a short task.
- Giving choices (which song, which exercise next) is justified by SDT autonomy and enjoyment, but **not** by a claim that choice improves motor learning, since that claim is now doubtful.
- Showing progress over time (range expanding, average miss shrinking week on week) targets competence and self-efficacy. Progress over time is more robust than a single-session score, which fluctuates.
- Relatedness is the SDT need a solo app meets least. Singing with or for others (rounds, songs for family events, sharing results with a coach) is a way to meet it.

### Gaps
- No controlled trial found of an app-based intervention for self-identified "non-singers".
- The exact effects of Berglin et al. (2022) and the details of Whidden's and Knight's findings could not be retrieved in full.
- No data on music performance anxiety specifically in **private, solo app practice**, as opposed to performing for others.

## 5. Gamification in language and music apps (streaks, XP, leaderboards): benefits, harms, designs that support intrinsic motivation; weekly vs daily streaks; forgiveness mechanics

### Takeaway
Gamification has **small-to-moderate average benefits** ([A], Sailer & Homner 2020). The motivational and behavioural effects are **less stable in rigorous studies**, and **leaderboards and badges can erode intrinsic motivation over time** (Hanus & Fox 2015). Expected, engagement-contingent rewards reliably undermine free-choice intrinsic motivation (Deci et al. 1999). Streaks do motivate: intact streaks increase continued engagement. But a **broken** streak demotivates, especially when the user blames themselves, and that harm is **reduced when the streak can be repaired** (Silverman & Barasch 2023). Habit research shows **one missed day does not derail habit formation** (Lally 2010), which argues for forgiving streak designs. Most specific Duolingo numbers are company-reported or passed through aggregators [C]. **No peer-reviewed comparison of weekly vs daily streaks was found.**

### Cited Findings
- [A] Sailer & Homner (2020, *Educational Psychology Review*), meta-analysis of gamification in learning: **cognitive g = 0.49, motivational g = 0.36, behavioural g = 0.25**. The cognitive effect held up in high-rigour studies, but the **motivational and behavioural effects were less stable**. — [ERIC EJ1245270](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1245270); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/335189630_The_Gamification_of_Learning_a_Meta-analysis). *A search summary also said "reward-and-status mechanics alone were far less effective than challenge, meaningful goals and narrative". That wording came from a secondary blog ([Cogn-IQ](https://www.cogn-iq.org/blog/gamified-learning/)) and was not confirmed against the paper, so treat it as unverified.*
- [A] A 2023 meta-analysis and systematic review (*Educational Technology Research and Development*): gamification **enhanced intrinsic motivation and perceived autonomy and relatedness, but had minimal impact on perceived competence**. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-023-10337-7)
- [A] Hanus & Fox (2015, *Computers & Education* 80:152–161): **16-week** semester comparing a course with leaderboard and badges to the same course without them. The gamified group showed **lower intrinsic motivation, satisfaction and empowerment over time** and **lower final exam scores**, with the effect on exam scores mediated by intrinsic motivation. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/265644737_Assessing_the_effects_of_gamification_in_the_classroom_A_longitudinal_study_on_intrinsic_motivation_social_comparison_satisfaction_effort_and_academic_performance); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Assessing-the-effects-of-gamification-in-the-A-on-Hanus-Fox/dff76a9862467d426113ec530f83942016ae3a97)
- [A] Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999, *Psychological Bulletin* 125:627–668): 128 experiments. **Engagement-contingent (d = −0.40), completion-contingent (d = −0.36) and performance-contingent (d = −0.28)** rewards undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation, and all *expected* tangible rewards undermined self-reported interest. The finding was disputed at the time by Cameron and colleagues in the same issue. — [PubMed 10589297](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297); [PDF](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf); comment: [PubMed 10589298](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589298)
- [A] Silverman & Barasch (2023, *Journal of Consumer Research* 49(6):1095), seven studies. Logged **intact streaks increase subsequent engagement relative to broken streaks**, and this depends on **how behaviour is shown in the log**, independent of actual past behaviour. The demotivating effect of a broken streak is **amplified when people attribute the break to themselves** and **reduced when the streak can be "repaired"**. — [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/49/6/1095/6623414); [INSEAD Knowledge](https://knowledge.insead.edu/marketing/consumer-streaks-are-motivating-key-keeping-them-alive); [CU Boulder news](https://www.colorado.edu/business/news/2023/04/20/research-streaks-marketing-tech-barasch)
- [A] Lally et al. (2010, *European Journal of Social Psychology*): **96 volunteers** did a chosen daily behaviour for 12 weeks. The median time to reach automaticity was **66 days** with wide variation, and **missing one opportunity did not materially affect habit formation**. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.674); [University of Surrey interview with Lally](https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/does-it-really-take-66-days-form-habit-we-asked-expert-dr-pippa-lally)
- [A] Singh, Murphy, Maher & Smith (2024, *Healthcare* 12(23):2488), systematic review and meta-analysis of health-habit formation: **median 59–66 days, mean 106–154 days, range 4–335 days**. Frequency and timing of practice and context were determinants. — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9032/12/23/2488); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/01/250124151347.htm)
- [C] Duolingo (company-reported, via secondary sources because the Duolingo blog was blocked): learners offered the **Weekend Amulet** were **4% more likely to return a week later** and **5% less likely to lose their streak**. A **streak wager** raised **Day-14 retention by about 14%**. Learners with a **7+ day streak were 2.4× more likely to return the next day**, and over 5 million users have streaks of a year or more. — [Yu-kai Chou](https://yukaichou.com/gamification-study/master-the-art-of-streak-design-for-short-term-engagement-and-long-term-success/); [StriveCloud](https://www.strivecloud.io/blog/gamification-examples-boost-user-retention-duolingo); [Duolingo blog (blocked)](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals/). *These are internal A/B results or correlations with strong self-selection. A widely repeated claim that streaks lifted Duolingo's retention "from 12% to 55%" comes from an aggregator and could not be traced to a primary source, so treat it as unreliable.*
- [C] Streak anxiety: commentary describes motivation shifting from "I enjoy learning" to "I'm afraid to break the chain", loss-framed notifications ("your streak is about to die"), and guilt about broken streaks. — [Institute of Network Cultures, Jan 2026](https://networkcultures.org/blog/2026/01/19/baby-please-dont-break-the-streak/); [UX Magazine](https://uxmag.com/articles/the-psychology-of-hot-streak-game-design-how-to-keep-players-coming-back-every-day-without-shame); [NerdSip blog](https://nerdsip.com/blog/gamification-gone-wrong-when-streaks-become-the-point). *Essays and anecdote, not controlled evidence. A "2019 Apple Watch study" mentioned in one summary could not be verified.*

### Inferences
- A **weekly streak** (e.g., "sang on at least 3 days this week") matches the habit and consolidation evidence better than a strict daily streak. One missed day does not hurt habit formation (Lally), and rest is compatible with vocal health (Q2). It also reduces the self-blame that Silverman & Barasch show amplifies the demotivation from a broken streak. This is an inference; no study compares weekly and daily streaks directly.
- Add **repair or forgiveness**: a missed week can be "mended" by an extra session. Silverman & Barasch found repairability reduces the harm of a broken streak.
- Log presentation matters. Showing cumulative totals ("32 days sung, 3 h 10 min of singing, range +4 semitones") keeps the record intact even after a gap. Showing only the current streak makes each break conspicuous.
- Avoid engagement-contingent rewards such as points for opening the app, and **avoid leaderboards**. Hanus & Fox found leaderboards harmful, and a solo adult beginner who is anxious about being judged is the user most likely to be hurt by social comparison. Informational competence feedback (cents improvement, range growth) fits SDT.
- Notifications, if the PWA ever adds them, should use gain framing ("5 minutes today keeps your voice warmed up") rather than loss framing.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study of weekly vs daily streaks.
- No peer-reviewed study of gamification in **singing** or music-practice apps specifically.
- Duolingo's primary data are not public. Its blog, the main public source, was not reachable in this session.

## 6. Goal-based pathways: what adult beginners want and how an app could tailor paths

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed data on adult beginners' **specific** singing goals (karaoke, choir audition, singing to children, church, musical theatre) is **thin**. What exists shows adults take up singing and music mainly for **enjoyment, personal growth, wellbeing and social connection**, not careers. Goal-based paths are therefore a reasonable, but mostly pedagogy-based, design choice.

### Cited Findings
- [A] Smith, Kleinerman & Cohen (2022, *Psychology of Music*): **48 adults (mean age 60.8) who began voice lessons after age 40**. Most said **enjoyment and personal growth** motivated them. Over **90%** reported physical (e.g., breathing) and mental-health benefits, and **67%** reported positive changes in social relations despite the one-to-one format. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/03057356211030992); [PubMed 35449604](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35449604/)
- [A] Roulston, Jutras & Kim (2015, *International Journal of Music Education*), interviews with adult instrument learners: adults began or resumed lessons for **skill development and personal pleasure**. The ensemble or social side of music-making is an important motivator for continuing, and adults value achievement and relatedness. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0255761415584291); Roulston (2010) "There is no end to learning" cited in the same results
- [A] Leahy & Smith (2021, *IJME*): self-directed learning of adult music students, comparing teacher approaches with student needs. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0255761421991596)
- [C] Studio and marketing sources: most new adult voice students "aren't chasing a stage career" but want a skill that feels good to practise. — [music-lessons.ca](https://www.music-lessons.ca/research/voice-lessons-statistics/); [ModernTone Studios](https://moderntonestudios.com/blog/5-reasons-you-should-take-voice-lessons-as-an-adult/)
- [A] Adult non-singers' blocks are usually social and emotional (Abril 2007, the Finnish intervention study, Whidden). Goals framed around **safe social singing** (singing with family, at events, in a group) address the underlying fear. — see Q4 citations

### Inferences
- A short onboarding question such as "What would you like to be able to do?" could reorder existing content without new lessons. Possible options:
  - "Sing Happy Birthday confidently": Happy Birthday first, plus leaps practice, since its octave leap on "Happy **birth**day dear…" is the hard part.
  - "Sing with my kids or grandkids": nursery songs (Twinkle, Mary Had a Little Lamb, Row Row) first, with a focus on a comfortable key.
  - "Sing in church or at community events": Amazing Grace and Waltzing Matilda, plus longer holds and breath work.
  - "Join a choir": rounds (Are You Sleeping, Row Row Row Your Boat), holding a line against another part, and a later part-singing unit.
  - "Karaoke and pop": public-domain constraints limit song choice, so emphasise range and the sirens and leaps skills.
- Rounds already in the song list are a cheap route to **relatedness** and a real choir skill: the app plays the other entry on piano while the user holds their part.
- Goals make **progress meaningful** (SDT competence; the "meaningful goals" point in the gamification literature, with the caveat noted in Q5).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed survey found of what adult beginner **singers** specifically want (karaoke, choir, parenting, church and so on), or of how goal-tailored paths affect retention in an app.
- Australia-specific data on adult singing goals was not found.

## 7. Implications for a self-guided singing app

### Takeaway
For Note by Note, the evidence points to five design changes:
- **Measure learning on a later day**, not only within the session.
- **Space and mix** practice once the basics are in place.
- **Fade the live pitch trace and the guide tone** as the user improves.
- **Target specific errors** rather than repeating whole exercises.
- **Build motivation around competence and forgiving habits**, not loss-framed streaks or leaderboards.

Most of these are cheap to add to the current feature set.

### Cited Findings: concrete design implications and the evidence each rests on
1. **Default session of about 10–15 min with a clear "done for today" ending.** Warm-up (3 min), plus one new or current lesson, plus a 2–3 item review block, plus one song line. Gently suggest stopping after about 2 new lessons in a day. *Evidence:* spacing and 24-h consolidation (Simmons 2012, [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ951332)); practice time not predicting retention (Duke et al. 2009, [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429408328851)); teaching convention of 15–20 min [B/C] ([VoiceLessons](https://www.voicelessons.com/blog/how-long-should-you-do-vocal-exercises-guide)); Duolingo bingers churning [C].
2. **"Yesterday check" at the start of each session.** Re-test the hardest exercise from the last session with no guide tone, and use **that** score, not same-session scores, to decide mastery. Tell users why ("today's score shows what stuck"). *Evidence:* performance vs learning distinction (Maas et al. 2008, [ASHA](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1058-0360(2008/025))); sleep consolidation (Simmons & Duke 2006, [UT CML](https://cml.music.utexas.edu/research/skill-learning-and-procedural-memory)); fluency illusion under blocked practice (Carter & Grahn 2016, [Grahn lab](https://www.jessicagrahn.com/cartergrahn-2016.html)).
3. **Mastery gate with a second-day confirmation.** Unlock the next lesson when about 80–85% of notes land within tolerance, confirmed on a later day. Otherwise offer an easier variant, such as a wider tolerance, a slower tempo or a smaller interval. Never hard-lock: offer "try the next one anyway" for autonomy. *Evidence:* mastery learning ES ≈ 0.52 (Kulik et al. 1990, [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543060002265)); challenge point ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Challenge_point_framework)); 85% as a **theoretical** anchor only (Wilson et al. 2019, [Nature Comms](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-12552-4)). *The thresholds are a tunable starting point, not validated values.*
4. **Adaptive difficulty dials, changed one at a time:** tolerance in cents (start generous, tighten after success, loosen after repeated misses), tempo, interval size and range width. *Evidence:* challenge point; systematic tempo variation by expert practicers (Duke et al. 2009); wide-range training (Pfordresher & Greenspon 2025, [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10298649241289542)).
5. **Randomise "match a note" and "echo game" targets across the singer's full measured range**, eventually about an octave, rather than a narrow comfortable band. Keep the first sessions mid-range so early attempts succeed. *Evidence:* only octave-range training improved single-pitch matching (Pfordresher & Greenspon 2025); early success for identity change (Whidden 2010, via [ERIC EJ1269707](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1269707.pdf)).
6. **Block first, interleave later.** Introduce each new skill blocked. Review passed skills in **mixed order** ("Mixed review": a step pattern, then a leap, then an arpeggio, then a hold). Explain that mixed practice feels harder but sticks better. *Evidence:* CI meta-analyses (Czyż et al. 2024, medium effects on retention and transfer SMD 0.55, [Sci Rep](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-65753-3), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39205981/)), which are contested ([Educ Psychol Rev 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10043-1)); challenge point (novices need lower CI first); increasing-CI schedule (Mathias & Goldman 2025, [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00224294231222801)).
7. **Spaced review queue for passed lessons and song lines**, at roughly 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days, stretching after a pass and shrinking after a miss. Surface one or two due items per session. *Evidence:* spacing meta-analysis (Cepeda et al. 2006, [York U](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/CPVWR2006.html)); half-life regression (Settles & Meeder 2016, [ACL](https://aclanthology.org/anthology-files/pdf/P/P16/P16-1174.pdf)).
8. **Fade the concurrent visual feedback.** Add "blind" rounds: hide the live voice trace while singing and show it straight afterwards as terminal feedback. Increase the share of blind rounds as accuracy improves. Keep full live feedback for users whose accuracy is still low. *Evidence:* 10 weeks of concurrent visual feedback improved training performance, but retention **converged with controls** after removal (Paney & Tharp 2021, [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334113826_The_effect_of_concurrent_visual_feedback_on_adult_singing_accuracy)); visual feedback helped low-accuracy learners most (Berglin et al. 2022, [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/03057356211026730)); the 2026 review on guidance dependency vs terminal feedback ([Frontiers 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1920074/full)); reduced feedback frequency principle (Maas et al. 2008).
9. **Fade the guide tone in headphones mode.** Guide on the first note or bar only, then drop out, which moves towards call-and-response and unaccompanied singing as the user improves. *Evidence:* auditory-feedback training shows **mixed** benefits (Berglin et al. 2022); the guidance-dependency concern applies to any concurrent aid (Frontiers 2026). *This is an inference: no singing study was found that directly tests fading a unison guide tone.*
10. **"Fix this spot" after each result.** Highlight the worst note or interval (and whether it was flat or sharp), offer a 3-repetition micro-drill of just that fragment at a slower tempo, then re-run the whole line. *Evidence:* the best practicers find the source of an error, fix it immediately and vary tempo (Duke et al. 2009, [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429408328851)).
11. **Ask users to guess before showing the score** ("Was that on, flat or sharp?"). This builds the internal error detection a solo learner needs, and self-estimate accuracy could itself be tracked as a skill. *Evidence:* inference from error-detection skill in expert practicers (Duke et al. 2009) and the self-regulated learning and metacognition framing of the 2026 review; not directly tested in singing.
12. **Word instructions with an external focus**, for example "aim the sound at the line", "send it to the far wall", "glide the dot up to the target". Avoid "feel your larynx or throat". *Evidence:* Atkins & Duke 2013 with untrained singers ([PDF](https://acda-publications.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/IJRCS/volumefour/ijrcs4_2_atkinsduke.pdf)); external-focus meta-analysis, learning g = 0.58 (Chua et al. 2021, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34843301/)), probably inflated.
13. **Keep the weekly streak and make it forgiving.** Frame it as "sing on 3+ days this week". Allow repair (an extra session the following week mends a missed week), treat rest days as part of training, and never use loss-framed copy ("your streak died"). *Evidence:* broken streaks demotivate, especially with self-blame, and repair reduces the harm (Silverman & Barasch 2023, [JCR](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/49/6/1095/6623414)); a missed day does not derail habit formation (Lally 2010, [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.674)); Weekend Amulet data [C].
14. **Lead with cumulative and competence metrics, not the streak.** Examples: total days sung, total singing minutes (voiced seconds from the pitch detector), range growth in semitones, and the trend of average miss in cents over weeks. **No leaderboards and no points for opening the app.** *Evidence:* leaderboards and badges reduced intrinsic motivation and exam scores (Hanus & Fox 2015, [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/265644737_Assessing_the_effects_of_gamification_in_the_classroom_A_longitudinal_study_on_intrinsic_motivation_social_comparison_satisfaction_effort_and_academic_performance)); engagement-contingent rewards d = −0.40 on intrinsic motivation (Deci et al. 1999, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297)); SDT competence (Evans 2015, [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1029864914568044)); gamification's motivational effects are small and unstable (Sailer & Homner 2020, [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1245270)).
15. **Onboarding for "I can't sing" users.** One screen of honest facts: many people think they can't sing, most sing reasonably in tune when tested, and pitch-matching is a trainable coordination skill, not a fixed trait. Stress that nothing is recorded. Make the first task one they can win. *Evidence:* ≈60% self-report vs ≈10–15% measured poor singers (Pfordresher & Brown 2007, [PDF](http://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf)); 17% self-define as tone deaf (Sloboda et al. 2005, [Wiley](https://nyaspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1196/annals.1360.018)); anxiety and judgement (Abril 2007); adult non-singers can progress (Finnish study, [European Publisher](https://www.europeanpublisher.com/en/article/10.15405/ejsbs.149)).
16. **Voiced-time meter and a gentle rest nudge.** Count actual phonation seconds per session. After about 10–15 minutes of voiced time, or when the user reports strain, suggest a break. Do not present this as a medical limit. *Evidence:* dosimetry concepts and the lack of an established threshold (NCVS, [NCVS](https://ncvs.org/strategies-for-safety-thresholds-of-phonation-for-performers-via-dosimetry/)); fatigue rising after 30-min loading tasks ([J Voice 2024 review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199724000250)); rest breaks extending singing before fatigue in karaoke amateurs ([J Voice 2003](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199703000389)).
17. **Goal-based paths from a single onboarding question**, used to reorder the existing songs and lessons and changeable at any time. Examples: Happy Birthday confidence, singing with kids, church or community, choir readiness through rounds, general enjoyment. *Evidence:* adults are motivated by enjoyment, personal growth, wellbeing and social connection (Smith et al. 2022, [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/03057356211030992); Roulston et al. 2015, [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0255761415584291)); SDT autonomy. *Thin evidence on specific goals.*
18. **Relatedness features that fit a solo, private app:** round-singing with the app playing the other entry; a "sing for someone" goal; the existing "Copy results for Claude" framed as having a coach. *Evidence:* the social and ensemble side sustains adult learners (Roulston et al. 2015); relatedness is an SDT need (Evans 2015); 67% of adult voice students reported social benefits (Smith et al. 2022).
19. **Offer choice for enjoyment, not as a learning lever.** Offer 2–3 recommended next activities, but do not rely on "learner control" to improve learning. *Evidence:* bias-corrected meta-analyses show self-controlled practice and enhanced-expectancy benefits were exaggerated (McKay et al. 2022, [Meta-Psychology](https://open.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/article/view/2803); McKay et al. 2025, [Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1750984X.2023.2207255)). Autonomy still matters for motivation under SDT.
20. **Enrich the results screen with precision and trend.** Alongside average miss in cents and flat or sharp tendency, add **consistency** (spread) and a **week-over-week trend**, and include both in "Copy results for Claude" so the AI coach can tell systematic bias from scatter. *Evidence:* the accuracy vs precision distinction in poor-pitch singing (Pfordresher et al. 2010, [PDF](http://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher_etal_2010_JASA.pdf)); self-efficacy through visible progress (Frontiers 2026 review).

### Inferences
- Items 2, 8, 10 and 13 are probably the highest value for the least effort. They change how existing lessons are sequenced, scored and displayed, without new content.
- The biggest risk in the current design is **guidance dependency**. A beginner who always sings with the live trace and a unison guide tone may do well in the app and worse without it. Fading both, and measuring on unaided next-day trials, addresses this directly.
- The weekly streak is already better aligned with the evidence than Duolingo-style daily streaks. The main gains now are repair mechanics and making cumulative progress the headline.

### Gaps
- None of the specific numeric parameters (80–85% gate, tolerance ladder, 10–15 min voiced-time nudge, review intervals) is validated for adult beginner singers. They are evidence-informed defaults that need tuning from real usage.
- No controlled studies of singing apps themselves, including gamified ones, were found, so every app-level recommendation is extrapolated from lab studies of instrumental learning, speech motor learning, general motor learning or other domains.
